# Realistic Physics

A no-engine 2D physics sandbox rewritten in Rust. The simulation is kept separate from the renderer so the physics model can be tested, tuned, and shipped on more than one platform, including the web.

**[Play it in your browser](https://azizjonkasimov.github.io/realistic-human-body-deformation-physics/)**: nothing to download, and the simulation runs entirely on your own device. To keep it one tap away, [install it as an app](#install-it-as-an-app) on your phone or computer.

**[Fight](https://azizjonkasimov.github.io/realistic-human-body-deformation-physics/fight/)**: the fighting game built on the engine, on the same site: two side-on boxers step in, jab and cross to the head or the body, block high or low, duck, and lean back, and get up off the floor while the referee counts, against a computer opponent over three rounds or with a friend. Its code is [its own repository](https://github.com/AzizjonKasimov/realistic-physics-fight) (see [Fight](#fight)).

**[How it was made](https://azizjonkasimov.github.io/work/body-physics/)**: the story on the author's site, of building it with AI coding agents and checking its injuries against published research.

See [docs/VISION.md](docs/VISION.md) for the original project description and agreed direction.
See [docs/DESTRUCTION_ARCHITECTURE.md](docs/DESTRUCTION_ARCHITECTURE.md) for the research-backed simulation architecture the Rust prototype is moving toward.

## Versions

The game shows its version, such as v0.7, on the browser's start screen and faintly in its bottom-left corner, so anyone who watched a video of it can tell whether the game has changed since. The start screen also lists what is new in that version, and [CHANGELOG.md](CHANGELOG.md) lists every version. Only the newest version is online. `realistic_physics.exe --version` prints the version without opening the game, and the YouTube Shorts made from the app show it at the top of every frame.

When a change that players would notice reaches `main`, bump the version in `Cargo.toml` in the same change and add its entry at the top of `CHANGELOG.md`, headed `## v0.8 (YYYY-MM-DD)`, with each change as a plain `- ` sentence, since the start screen shows them as written:

- the middle number for a new feature or a big change, such as a new tool, a new kind of injury, or a step towards the fighting game (0.7.0 to 0.8.0, shown as v0.8);
- the last number for a fix or tuning (0.7.0 to 0.7.1, shown as v0.7.1).

Docs, tests, and tools leave the version alone. `cargo test` and `tools\build_web.ps1` stop when `Cargo.toml` and `CHANGELOG.md` disagree. The deploy tags each version the first time it goes live (`v0.7.0`), so `git checkout v0.7.0` or `.\tools\compare.ps1 -Ref v0.7.0` gets the game that a video of that version showed. Versions before v0.7 were numbered afterwards from the history, and their tags mark each one's last commit.

## Current Rust Milestone

- Rust Cargo project with a reusable `realistic_physics` simulation library.
- Cross-platform `macroquad` app for windowing, input, and rendering, built as a desktop app and as a WebAssembly browser version that GitHub Pages hosts.
- On-screen control buttons that work with mouse and touch, so the browser version is playable on phones and tablets. The body is drawn fitted between the status chips at the top and the buttons at the bottom in any window shape, so no control covers it, and redrawn to fit whenever the window or the buttons change, hurt or not. It is only drawn larger or smaller: on every screen it is simulated at the size the tests play it, 561.6 px tall in a 1280 px wide world (`world_frame`), and the pointer is mapped back into that world (`src/view.rs`), so a blow hurts it alike on a big monitor, a laptop, or a phone. The simulation steps at 60 Hz, but the app spreads each frame's pointer motion over its steps and draws the body, bones, and tool between the last two steps, so they move as smoothly as the screen refreshes, 120 or 144 Hz included.
- Physical tissue (`src/simulation/tissue.rs`): each point of skin and muscle weighs what its share of real tissue weighs, and each spring and triangle is as stiff as the tissue measures. Skin shears at about 0.2 MPa and turns 70 times stiffer once stretched about a quarter, as its collagen straightens (a J-shaped curve); relaxed muscle shears at 4-6 kPa and stiffens as it stretches; and the fat under the skin holds it to the muscle at 7.5 kPa, stiffening as it slides and tearing once it has slid six times its thickness. Living skin rests stretched, about 8%, so a cut gapes on its own, and the body's outline holds that stretch as the skin round the back of a real body does. The figures and their sources are in [docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md](docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md#physical-tissue).
- The solver (`src/simulation/soft.rs`) is rebuilt from Box2D v3's soft step (Erin Catto, MIT). Each 60 Hz step runs as four substeps; every spring, triangle, tie, and joint is a damped spring solved implicitly from its stiffness and damping ratio, so the body is as stiff however many substeps run; and each starts from the force it carried a substep before (warm starting), so a held load is carried from the start. What tears in a substep tears before that, so a torn tie gives none of its pull. The body's contacts with the tools, with bone, and with itself are still projected by position between the solver's passes; where the body presses into itself, it is pushed apart no faster than Box2D's contact speed (3 m/s), so a flap of cut skin falling against the body is not thrown off.
- The body stands on its own skeleton in a fighting guard (`src/simulation/stance.rs`), fists up beside its head and feet apart on the floor. Every point of skin and muscle keeps its place on the bones under it, the two nearest counting by how near they lie, so the flesh moves and turns with them and its weight hangs on them. The muscles across each joint hold the guard as a person's do: as stiffly, as damped, and at most as strongly as measured (rebuilt from Box2D's motor joint, with stiffness and damping in physical units as Jolt's spring parts take them; the figures and sources are in [docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md](docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md#the-standing-body)). So a bat to the head snaps it aside and rocks the body, a tap knocks a fist aside and the arm brings it back into the guard, and a blow hard enough overpowers them. The hips are held up, since the body does not keep its own balance yet, and the feet stand where they are, each held as the friction under a foot bearing its share of the body's weight holds it (a dry floor's 0.6 of it sideways): pushed harder, a foot slides, lifted harder than that weight it comes up, and a leg hanging limp or torn at the hip, knee, or ankle stands on nothing. So when a punch or a lean shifts the hips, the legs lean in under them, the far one straightening and the near one bending, each leg's hip, knee, and ankle aimed so that its thigh and shin reach from the hip to the foot as two sides of a triangle, the foot kept flat, and shifted far to one side the hips sink, as a person's do, so each leg still reaches its foot; held up at the hips with their feet bearing no weight, the legs swung from them like a pendulum, the feet sliding 40 cm and one rising off the floor. It stands under real gravity, 9.81 m/s² at its own scale, as drawn: it settles under its weight for a second and a half before play, taking that weight over the first half second, while its muscles, joints, and the hold on its hips learn it, and each joint holds its bones' ends together as a hinge does, so a bone turns about its joint but cannot sag round it. Every joint holds each of its two bones together with the flesh held to it, as one piece (`src/simulation/sides.rs`), as Box2D's revolute joint holds two bodies, and the muscles across it, a punch's and a lean's, the hold on the hips, and the feet's holds turn and move those same pieces, as the hips' and the legs' muscle ties pull their bones: a point of flesh held seven tenths or more to a bone moves wholly with it, one held between three and seven tenths blends between its two bones, and skin come away from the muscle under it, as a cut's flap does, moves with neither. Held on their light bones alone, with the flesh, most of a limb's weight, hanging on them by soft ties, the joints came apart whenever a limb was pressed on bone and flesh at once, as another fighter's arm presses it: punches thrown fast one after another tore the puncher's wrist and elbow, and with the arms alone holding their flesh, the lower back. A trunk leaning over feet that stand on the floor bends about its lower back, the hips staying where they stand. A hand or a foot a tool presses is held at its wrist or ankle on its light bone alone while it is pressed, since a tool shoves what it meets out of its way whatever it weighs: held with its flesh to a leg as heavy as the leg's flesh, a foot a bat swept along the floor turned half round about its ankle and came out of its skin. A joint beside a broken bone holds that bone alone, as the flesh round a break holds its pieces: held with its flesh, a forearm broken by a hammer dragged the flesh against its pieces' push, and the elbow tore apart. A broken collarbone, upper arm, or forearm leaves its arm hanging and a broken thigh or shin its leg, as the limb's muscles let go: a person cannot hold a broken arm up. A joint torn apart still keeps its bones within a few centimetres, as a dislocated joint's capsule and muscles do. The muscles that cross each elbow (brachialis, biceps, brachioradialis, triceps) are cords from the upper arm to the forearm, at the places they attach in a real arm (`src/simulation/tendons.rs`, rebuilt from MuJoCo's tendon with a length limit and solved as Box2D's distance joint holds its limit; the figures are in [docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md](docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md#the-muscles-across-the-elbow)): slack through the elbow's whole range, they leave the arm free to hang or straighten, and once the elbow tears or the forearm is smashed they keep the forearm hanging from the upper arm and its bones inside it, pulling no harder than each muscle's measured strength and tearing once stretched as far as a relaxed muscle tears. The flesh round each elbow rests as a relaxed elbow's does, part way open, so a broken arm drops out of the guard and hangs at the side rather than being held up by the fold of its own flesh.
- The fight scenarios, captures, and the viewer can stand two of these front-view bodies side by side in the same world (the app shows one; two fighters box on the Fight page, seen from the side), 0.52 body heights apart (`FIGHTER_SPACING`), the second built as the first's mirror image (`World::mirrored`, `create_fighters_at`), and each keeps its own blood, limp limbs, punches, and broken bones (`BodyState`); a body built as a mirror image breaks as the first's mirror image. Every tool strikes either one. Where they touch, their skins push each other, with the muscle the fat ties under the skin as one column of flesh (pushing the skin alone, two arms driven into each other dragged the skin off its muscle until it tore), both pushed together each pass so they stay mirror images (`src/simulation/outline.rs`). Where their limbs meet they also press on each other's bones through the flesh between them (`src/simulation/clash.rs`): each bone of an arm or a leg, and the skull, is as thick as the flesh round it, and two bodies' bones nearer than that are pushed apart as Box2D pushes two shapes apart, on the bone and its flesh as one piece, in the same pass as the muscles that drive them, so two arms driven into each other stop or glance off each other instead of passing through. Skin pushing on skin alone could not stop a bone: two fighters punching a few steps apart drove their guards elbow into elbow until the bones crossed, the skin crumpled, and bone showed.
- The fighters throw punches (`src/simulation/punch.rs`): a straight punch with the arm nearer the other fighter, out to its side at the height of the other's head, and back into its guard, driven by its own muscles at their measured strength. The body goes into it as a cross's does, seen from the front: its hips shift 15 cm toward the target over 0.3 s, the lower back holding the trunk upright on them (a cross's rear hip goes 28 cm forward and its fist moves for 0.31 s, the elbow straightening in the last tenth; Cheraghi et al. 2014), the arm going out in the last twelfth of a second, so the fist lands about 0.3 s after the key at about 9.6 m/s, inside an untrained man's 5.7 to 11.1 m/s (Adamec et al. 2021), and the body comes back upright over a second. The fist follows the smoothest path to the end of its reach, as a reaching hand does (minimum jerk, Flash and Hogan 1985), each joint's muscles aiming it there. While an arm punches, the lower back turns the trunk, the feet on the floor taking its push back, and the girdle, shoulder, elbow, and wrist each turn their bone with its flesh against the bone before with its own, the pieces the arm's joints hold together (rebuilt from Box2D's revolute joint's motor), so the light bones keep the heavy flesh they carry and the punch's reaction turns the body rather than flinging the arm's own bones; the neck holds the head still and the other shoulder keeps its guard by the head. The fist strikes the other fighter as the bare fist does (below): the same 2.9 kg behind it, knuckle face, and rules for bruising, splitting, and breaking, pressing on through the solver's substeps, and leaving the puncher's own body alone. What it gives the other body the punching arm loses, turning about its shoulder as one piece, and the blow presses back on the puncher's hand, bending its bone as far as it presses across it and breaking it past its strength, as a punch on a skull can break a bare-knuckle fighter's hand. A fist that meets something faster than 2 m/s turns back from there, as a punch that lands is pulled back: driven on to the pose it aimed at, a fist that met a guard ground on into the neck beyond; a fist pushed backward lands on nothing. A punching arm the other fighter's limb presses harder than 600 N gives way, unless that limb hangs limp from a break: its own muscles take it home, and a punch still on its way out turns back, the hips coming back from as far as they got, so two fighters punching at nearly the same moment bump guards and both punches come back. An arm that went limp from a broken bone throws no punch, and both fighters' punches pressed on the same step both throw (`InputState::punches`).
- Front-view mannequin body: a neutral, exactly mirror-symmetric figure defined in code (egg-shaped head, smooth torso, tapered limbs, mitten hands, simple feet, legs apart), built in the guard. The inside of each elbow is a narrow crease, so the forearm's flesh and the upper arm's meet there without being one piece and the elbow opens freely, and the flesh over the point of the elbow slides over the joint rather than being tied to either bone. Skin and muscle sheets are meshed to follow its outline (outline points, closer together along the crease, plus an interior hexagonal lattice, Delaunay-triangulated), with the muscle sheet inset just inside the skin, and the mesh mirrors across the midline too. Flesh is tied to bone and to other flesh only through flesh, never across a gap in the figure such as the armpit or the crease.
- Dynamic segmented bones built on the figure's own limb joints, so every limb bone runs down the middle of its limb, plus a low-resolution rib-cage proxy, two collarbones jointed to the top of the spine where the breastbone is, and hands and feet on wrist and ankle joints, attached to nearby muscle points, and connected by breakable bone joints.
- Bone joints can subluxate under traumatic stretch/overextension before full breakage, adding limited slack, weaker correction, and first-time local ligament/capsule tissue damage so dislocation exists between intact articulation and total separation.
- A break along a bone leaves the joints at its ends whole, as a broken forearm leaves the elbow; a break at a joint tears it, and what is left holds the pieces loosely. The two pieces of a break stay hinged together at the break, bending and shifting there as far as the periosteum and the muscle round it allow; they do not collide with each other at the break, which the hinge holds, so their ends can ride past each other as a displaced break's do, but they do where they fold onto each other further along.
- Three hand-held tools whose drawings match their collision shapes exactly: a baseball bat (blunt; the barrel lies across the swing), a double-edged knife (sharp; the tip leads), and a sledgehammer (heavy; a striking face leads). Each tool is a solid object with momentum held firmly in the hand: it moves with the pointer, easing only into sudden starts and stops, and once it touches the body the arm presses it in harder the farther the pointer is past it, up to what an arm can push whatever the tool weighs (half that behind a broad bat or hammer face), while the body pushes back. A bat or sledgehammer is held by its handle and turns no faster than it could be swung round the hands: carried with the button up it stays upright in the hand, swung it turns until its striking part leads with the handle across the swing, and flesh holds it nearly in line, so it slides along the body instead of swinging round into it. A knife turns at once to lead with its tip, follows its stroke in tissue, and withdraws when pulled back instead of flipping around in the wound. Each step's motion is swept so fast swings cannot skip thin limbs. Each tool swings with one fixed strength, the way a person would swing it: the knife and the bat in one hand, the heavier sledgehammer with both.
- A fourth tool, a bare fist, is the fist and forearm of someone punching the body from beside it. It strikes as a punch does: 2.9 kg behind it, as a puncher's fist, forearm, and the body behind them carry (Walilko et al. 2005), up to about 10 m/s, with its row of knuckles a face lying across its path; it turns no faster than a forearm swings round behind it, and pulled back it stays pointing at the body rather than turning round. Knuckles and the hand behind them give where they land, as wood and steel do not, so a fist meets bone more softly than a bat at the same speed (below) and pushes flesh aside more gently: a hard punch bruises and knocks the head aside on the neck, rarely splits the skin, and breaks no skull, forearm, or rib, as in bare-knuckle bouts (Muzzi et al. 2022). A blow on a rib meets the give of the rib cage it belongs to, a spring of 26.3 kN/m (`RIB_CAGE_STIFFNESS`, Lobdell et al. 1973), since ribs break as a chest is pressed in rather than by the force on one; met as bare bone, a punch broke a rib in about one run in six.
- The knife moves only through fibers it severs. Each fiber its tip or cutting edge reaches is cut when the knife's momentum or the hand's push overcomes it; otherwise the knife stops against it, so a light push rests on the skin and pressing harder cuts in, and it slides along fibers and bone it cannot cut. Skin is the tough layer; the muscle under it parts readily. Bone stops the blade without breaking. A knife put down onto the body goes into the flesh there and cuts its way along. In this front view the spine lies behind the chest and belly, so it does not stop a blade cutting the front of the trunk; ribs, pelvis, collarbones, and limb bones do.
- Knife cuts open exactly where the blade went. Each triangle of skin or muscle the blade passes through splits into two pieces along the blade's path, each holding the flesh on its own side of it; where the flesh on the two sides no longer meets, the points split, and a piece's corners across the cut become ghosts, copies of the points that carry the piece's shape but hold no flesh, so nothing touches them and only each piece's own side is drawn. The skin rests stretched, as living skin does, so its own tension then pulls each side back from the cut, which gapes along the blade's path and shows the muscle under cut skin, deep raw flesh inside cut muscle, and bone where the knife reached it. No tissue is lost, no piece is too small to stay steady, and a cut opens even just inside a limb's outline. The technique is rebuilt from the virtual node algorithm (Molino, Bao, and Fedkiw, 2004), as PhysBAM's 2D code builds it, with the cut embedded in each piece as Sifakis, Der, and Fedkiw (2007) do. The blade judges which fibers it crosses by where they lay when the step began as well as where they lie now, since it nudges the tissue just ahead of it.
- A bat, hammer, or fist gives its momentum to the tissue and bone it shoves, so it slows and stops in the body instead of sweeping through it, and the tissue keeps pushing back on it in every solver pass. Damage grows with the speed of the blow the way real blunt injuries do: bone breaks under the force of a blow, which grows with its speed (below), so a tool merely resting on a bone does not load it, while flesh is crushed and bruised in step with the blow's energy, so half the speed does a quarter of the damage. A slow push or tap leaves at most a small bruise, a firm blow bruises the flesh around where it lands, and only a hard swing breaks bone; breaking a bone takes much of the blow's speed, so the tool does not plow on through the limb. A broad face spreads the force, so a bat rarely splits the skin, and an arm takes a blow aimed past it. A bat or hammer gripped while inside the body passes through until it is clear, so it cannot appear in flesh and blast it apart. Flesh the tool runs past while the flesh around holds it back goes back out ahead of the tool, the side it came from, so a fast blow on a braced arm does not drag its skin back through the tool and tear it. Tools meet only what holds together: flesh torn away (all of whose mesh triangles have failed or torn, which the app draws as crushed flesh beneath the wound) is not there for them, and neither is the spine, which lies behind the trunk, so a tool moves through a wound as freely as through the air and meets only its edges and the bone left in it. Loose bone fragments pass through torn-away flesh too. A blunt tool's motion is divided into steps of a quarter of the mesh spacing, and each point's load comes from how far the tool pressed it over the whole step, so a blow does the same damage however finely its motion is divided.
- A blow presses on a bone with the peak force of a mass and a spring (`src/simulation/cover.rs`, the model Robinovitch, Hayes, and McMahon built for falls on the hip): the speed at which the tool meets the bone times the square root of the contact's stiffness and of the tool's and the struck limb's masses in series. So the force grows with the speed of a blow rather than its energy, a bone twice as strong takes a blow four times as hard, a light bat meets a limb with nearly all its mass while much of a heavy hammer's goes into shoving the limb aside, and the flesh over a bone softens the blow as a spring in series with it (about 27% less force through 30 mm, as hip tissue cuts the force of a fall). A fist is a spring in series too, its knuckles and hand about 40 kN/m, so a punch meets a bone with less force than a bat moving as fast ([docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md](docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md#punches)). The figure is a flat front view, so the flesh drawn between a tool and a bone is not always what covers it: the figure draws the trapezius above the collarbone, which in a real shoulder lies behind it, while the collarbone's top lies right under the skin. A bone therefore takes a blow from the moment the tool presses the skin over it in the figure, and how much the flesh softens it follows what covers that face of a real bone, from ultrasound and CT studies: about 8 mm over the collarbone, 30 mm of muscle outside the shin and 12 mm over its inner face, 25 mm over the upper arm, 40 mm outside the thigh. `anatomy_diagnostics` prints the flesh the figure draws over each bone beside the real one.
- Stress-based tearing from overstretched or high-impulse springs.
- Sharp cuts propagate from existing wound edges into adjacent stressed or fatigued skin springs under a per-step cap, so cuts can grow along local stress instead of appearing only as isolated threshold breaks; a tear that runs on from a cut opens as the cut does.
- Skin or muscle that a fast blow (a tool moving at least 800 px/s) tears by stretching splits open across the torn fiber, so it parts perpendicular to the pull as a laceration does, as in Müller et al.'s position-based tearing and Rapier's crack opening. A slow overload only parts the fiber, tissue crushed under a heavy load is torn away, and a fiber at the edge of a cut breaks as before.
- Sharp cut edges can delaminate nearby skin-to-muscle attachments under local load, creating capped skin-flap peeling and more physically driven exposure of the muscle layer.
- Fiber-aligned muscle springs now report separate rupture telemetry from cross-fiber muscle tears, with an opt-in damage-detail floor for later anisotropic tissue tuning.
- Soft-tissue springs accumulate persistent fatigue from repeated subcritical stretch/load, which lowers local tear thresholds and feeds muscle damage detail before full rupture.
- Damaged tissue can take a bounded permanent set during post-impact settling, so surviving springs keep small plastic stretch/crush deformation instead of always snapping back to their original rest shape; the default plastic rate is tuned through long-settle strike telemetry.
- Blunt and heavy contact now leaves persistent tissue contusion/crush state on impacted points, and contused springs locally soften and tear at lower load so repeated trauma changes material behavior instead of only tinting the surface.
- Failed muscle triangles can rupture into capped crush-bleeding sources, so heavy blunt/internal damage produces persistent fluid and wound evidence instead of only visual void shading.
- A low-resolution torso cavity pressure proxy groups internal muscle area constraints, builds bounded pressure/collapse state under deep compression, pushes back on surrounding tissue, and uses separate non-heavy pressure/load caps so medium blunt hits can bruise internal tissue without opening the capped internal rupture path reserved for heavier trauma.
- Anchored low-resolution organ proxies for lungs, liver, and spleen accumulate pressure/load/fragment damage and can rupture into capped internal bleeding when severe torso-cavity trauma supports it, when a sharp/deep striker explicitly penetrates an organ proxy, or when severe fractured-rib tip motion punctures a nearby organ proxy.
- Low-resolution major vessel paths follow nearby muscle anchors and can lacerate under deep sharp or heavy contact, feeding high-pressure wound sources rather than treating every wound as the same bleed. The arm arteries run down the inside of each arm between the bone and the skin, as the brachial artery does.
- Exposed muscle is a second mesh coupled to skin through breakable attachments, whose corrections leave no hidden motion behind, so a body left alone comes fully to rest.
- Parts of the body press against each other instead of passing through: the skin and muscle sheets are each bounded by one outline, and a point of it that is pushed behind another stretch of it (the inside of an arm driven against the chest, a hand against a thigh, one leg against the other) is pushed back out and pushes that stretch in. Only surfaces that face each other across a gap in the body's rest shape can touch, and flesh torn away no longer counts.
- A bone breaks where a load bends it most (`src/simulation/bending.rs`, NVIDIA Blast's stress solver reduced to one bone as a beam on its joints): a blow counts by how hard it bends the bone where it lands, so the same blow breaks a bone in the middle far more readily than beside a joint, a blow pressed along the bone, as a bat lying along a hanging forearm, bends it less than one across it, and a break comes under the hardest bending, not at a fixed point. A break twice the bone's strength or more also breaks a butterfly wedge out of the struck side. A load along a bone, as a blow on the point of the shoulder down the collarbone, bears far less than one across it (a quarter for the limbs' long bones, half for the curved collarbone), and a blow on a long bone's end beside a joint, or on the outer fifth of a collarbone, can crush the end, which takes about 1.7 times the force that breaks the shaft across its middle.
- Bones fracture at loaded contact points into recursive fragments, release nearby muscle-to-bone anchors, keep rotational inertia, and continue damaging tissue from broken ends. Most fractures stay closed: the bone ends tear the muscle right at the break and bruise the flesh around it while the skin and the bleeding stay inside. Only a break loaded far past the bone's strength shifts its pieces apart, chips a splinter, and tears out through the flesh as an open, bleeding fracture; the flesh around a splinter or butterfly wedge holds it in the limb, since a piece thrown clear of a limb is rare and comes from high-energy crashes, so broken bone stays in the body and at worst a broken end pokes out through the skin; how far past depends on the weapon, as in assault studies, so a bat's smooth, rounded barrel rarely breaks bone open and a sledgehammer more often, and the pieces of a closed break leave the skin whole: whole skin round a closed break holds its pieces in, as skin tents over a broken bone. Each bone is as strong as fracture tests make it: the collarbone breaks at about half a forearm's force, and the upper arm, shin, and thigh at 2.1 to 2.5 times it. Only blows, pieces of broken bone, and flesh pressed onto a bone break it; what a joint passes on strains the joint and the muscle on the bones but breaks no bone, and a knife notches a long bone without breaking it.
- The moving ends of an open break's pieces and chips can puncture intact skin from inside the body under impulse, and severe fractured-rib tips can puncture organ proxies, with capped telemetry separate from generic fragment-tissue tearing. A closed break's chips stay under the skin, since a piece through it would make the break an open one. A broken end damages flesh only by moving through it: a limb hanging limp carries its broken pieces and their flesh alike. Broken pieces move with what they lie in: their contacts with flesh and bone slow them against what they touch, not against the world, and a piece and the flesh it presses on part by their masses.
- Severe moving pieces of an open break can lacerate nearby major vessel paths through a capped swept-tip query (a closed break, which has not torn through the flesh, rarely injures an artery), opening the same pressure-wound system while keeping fragment-driven vascular injury separate from direct tool cuts.
- Open fractures create bone-anchored marrow bleeding sources that follow the broken fragment and leak through the persistent wound system instead of being only a one-frame particle burst.
- Runtime budgets, broad-phase spatial filtering, and low-energy fragment sleeping cap active fragment work, fragment-bone checks, fragment-pair checks, fragment-tissue checks, vessel lacerations, wound sources, and fluid particles so heavier destruction remains PC-real-time.
- Broken fragments now collide with nearby intact bones, push against them, and transfer load back into the skeleton instead of only interacting with tissue and other fragments.
- Slow fragment-to-intact-bone overlaps and late-settle near contacts add damping, friction, and resting support so debris can jam against remaining skeleton instead of sliding through or rattling endlessly.
- Fragment-pair contacts damp closing velocity, tangential sliding, and angular jitter so piles of debris settle instead of endlessly rattling.
- Slow fragment-pair overlaps receive extra resting-contact support so settled debris resists tiny sinking/rattling under sustained load without raising global solver iterations.
- Free fragments use radius-aware floor contacts with damping, friction, angular drag, and resting-contact telemetry so debris can settle against the environment instead of being only center-clamped.
- Recursive fracture density is tuned through explicit material controls for maximum fracture depth, generic/rib-specific minimum fragment length, and secondary fragment strength, with deterministic strike gates covering long-settle debris.
- Blood comes only out of wounds, at the rate of what each one cut (below), so no more blood ever shows than the body has lost: a fresh tear, break, or rupture opens a wound and bleeds from it, and under unbroken skin its blood goes into the flesh as a bruise. A cut artery's blood finds its way out of the nearest opening in the skin, as up a knife's track. Open wounds together let out at most 4 drops a step, and blood leaves a wound at most at 560 px/s, so a wound wells up and drips rather than bursting into a cloud. Each drop holds about 0.05 ml, falls under the same gravity as the body, and lands as a spot that runs together with the spots and pools it touches.
- Blood is drawn at its real size for a person the body's size (`src/simulation/blood.rs`): a falling drop as the sphere its blood makes, about 4.6 mm across (a pixel and a half at the scenarios' scale), a bead on the skin as wide as the skin it wets, a trail as the film a running bead leaves, and a pool as wide as its landing spots or as that much blood spreads on a floor (40 ml makes a pool about 19 cm wide; [docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md](docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md#how-injuries-look) has the sources), so four hard sledgehammer blows into one arm show the blood they let out, a couple of tablespoons, as trickles down the skin and spots on the floor, not a metre-wide pool. `World::blood_shown_ml` and `Stats::blood_shed_ml` keep the books: every drop's blood is shown, or counted as gone from view when a budget replaces it.
- Blood that wells out rather than spurting, from every wound but a freshly cut artery, clings to the skin and runs down it (`src/simulation/blood.rs`, rebuilt from the surface walkers of Overgrowth's blood): each drop sits in a skin triangle by barycentric weights, so it moves as the skin does, beads until it holds enough blood to run (more than one drop's worth), gives the film it leaves on the skin to its trail, merges with the drops it meets, and drips off where the body's outline turns under it. The trails stay on the skin and darken as they dry.
- Blood that lands stays where it lands and deposits capped, mergeable pools on the floor, drawn flat and glossy and drying dark, so bleeding leaves persistent physical evidence instead of disappearing as particle fade only.
- Persistent wound sources anchor to nearby moving tissue or bone features, bleed at the rate of what they cut on the surgical bleeding scale (torn skin and muscle ooze about 6 ml a minute, an open fracture's marrow about 25, and a cut artery about 250, which alone sprays at first; each drop drawn stands for about 0.05 ml), clot down over time, and reopen when later local load, stretching of the tissue around the clot, a blunt blow landing on it, or fresh damage at the same spot disturbs it; a clotted source keeps its place until the wound budget needs the slot, so a healed cut can bleed again when struck later.
- Wound leakage drains a finite normalized blood reserve, and remaining reserve feeds back into wound pressure/leak strength and passive tissue turgor so long severe bleeding does not behave like an infinite source or fully supported tissue.
- Wounds are drawn from the mesh itself, with no overlay marks: a knife cut is a thin line along the blade's path that opens into a gap between dark lips, with deep raw flesh inside where it cut the muscle; skin torn by stretching shows a line through the torn springs that fades as the tear gapes; open skin shows the muscle beneath as solid flesh with a thin dark rim where intact skin meets the opening, muscle crushed or torn out shows as the dark, pulped flesh behind it (a body keeps its depth under a wound, so a smashed arm never shows a hole through to the background), and bones lie beneath the flesh so they show only through openings or where a broken end sticks out.
- Visual damage diagnostics replay two knife cuts and a sledgehammer blow, check that what is drawn matches what the simulation did, and write a damage-focused SVG and a CSV so incision, wound-rim, exposed-muscle, lacerated-vessel, blood, and fracture rendering can be inspected without launching the app.
- Rust diagnostics, strike scenario playback, and simulation tests.
- Smoothly shaded skin: one mesh with per-point color for pressure, the redness a blow leaves, and the bruise as far as it shows yet, soft shading strips inside the silhouette for volume, and a thin outline. A fresh blow leaves a red mark at once, and its bruise darkens over the next quarter hour, as a fast blow's bruise does, then stays (`src/simulation/bruises.rs`), so a hit turns from red to purple while you watch rather than the instant it lands. Whole skin hides what lies under it; only an opening in it shows the flesh or bone beneath.
- Sound made from the simulation, with no recordings: after every step the game hears what happened and plays it. A bat or sledgehammer lands with a thud as loud and deep as the blow was fast, so a slow push barely makes a sound; a bone cracks as it breaks; flesh torn by a blow tears wetly; a cut artery's spurting blood spatters as it lands; air rushes past a swung tool, brighter as it goes faster; and the knife slices as long as it cuts. The sounds are synthesized when the app starts, the same way the project's videos get their sound, and `M` or the speaker button mutes them (see [Sound](#sound)).

## Realism Target

The project target is to get as close as practical to how real bodies are injured, checked against published injury research. Injuries show as they really are: blood, exposed tissue, torn skin and muscle, broken bones, bruises, and deformation appear whenever the simulation produces them, and they are not toned down. Nor are they exaggerated: the goal is realistic injury, not gore for its own sake. What is drawn follows what the simulation says happened, no more and no larger: blood shows as much as the wounds have lost and at the size it would really be, a fresh blow leaves a red mark and its bruise darkens over the next quarter hour, and broken bone stays in the limb. The visual damage diagnostic and the scenarios check this, as they check the injuries against research.

## Fight

The site's Fight page (`fight/`, opened by the Fight button beside Start on the start screen) is the fighting game built on this engine, with the [side-view fighters](#side-view-fighters): you play the left fighter, step in and back with `D` and `A`, hold `W` to lean back and `S` to duck, jab with `J` and cross with `K` (thrown while ducking, they go to the body), and hold `L` to block (while ducking, a low block over the body, which leaves the head open); a computer opponent, at a level picked on the page's start card (easy, normal, or hard), fights the right fighter until a friend takes it over with the arrows and `,` `.` `/`. A fight is three rounds of a minute. A fighter knocked down gets up on its own legs while the referee counts, the other waiting at the ropes on its own side, as a boxer waits in a neutral corner: up before ten, it boxes on once the count reaches eight (the mandatory eight count); still down at ten, it is knocked out; and a third knockdown ends the fight. A fall with no clean punch landed in the two seconds before it is a slip, and goes uncounted. Otherwise the fighter with more clean punches (landed on the head or body, not the arms) wins when the last round ends. Its code lives in its own repository, [realistic-physics-fight](https://github.com/AzizjonKasimov/realistic-physics-fight), under the PolyForm Strict License 1.0.0, and uses this engine as a library through a Cargo git dependency pinned to a commit: the bodies, their injuries, how they move, their drawing (`src/draw.rs`), and their sound (`src/sound/player.rs`) are the engine's, and the game adds the controls, the computer opponent, the rounds and the referee's count, the ring's edge, and the screen around the fight. It tells its fighters every move through the step's input (`InputState::moves`, up to `MOVES_A_STEP` a step), a held move's start and end too, since the step notes which arms already lie across the other fighter before any move changes them; it reads where each stands (`World::fighter_stands`), whether one is down, from the moment it goes down until it stands again (`World::fighter_fallen`), and whether it was knocked out and has not come to yet (`World::knocked_out`), what each is doing as the screen shows it, its punch on its way and whether it goes to the body, its step, how far into a lean, a duck, or a block, high or low, and whether it is down or getting up (`World::fighter_doing`, which its opponent watches), and which punches landed on the last step and on what, the head, the body, or an arm (`World::landed_punches`, which decide a bout on clean punches). The last two only read the world (`src/simulation/sight.rs`). A change the game needs in the engine is made here, under this repository's licenses and checks, and the game then moves its pin.

The site builds the game from its repository at the version [`web/fight.version`](web/fight.version) names (one line, a tag of the game's repository): the deploy checks that tag out and runs `tools\build_web.ps1 -Fight` on it. To change what the Fight page shows, tag the game's new version, change that line, and push it to `main`. Locally, with the game checked out beside this repository:

```powershell
.\tools\build_web.ps1 -Fight ..\realistic_physics_fight -Serve
```

builds the sandbox with the Fight page in it and serves both at <http://localhost:8080> (the Fight page at <http://localhost:8080/fight/>). Without `-Fight` the build leaves the Fight button out. `.\tools\touch_check.ps1 -Fight` then plays the Fight page's buttons with real fingers on a phone in headless Edge (`-Size 844x390` sideways): STEP IN, JAB tapped by a second finger while BLOCK is held, CROSS, then DUCK and LEAN BACK held, and prints the frame cost the page measured (`?stats`). `.\tools\pad_check.ps1` plays it with fake game controllers in place of the browser's Gamepad API: a DualSense named on the start card and starting the fight with cross, walking, jabbing, blocking, ducking, and leaning back as the left fighter; an Xbox controller taking the right fighter; a clean punch rumbling both (the one hit harder); a key turning the buttons back to keys, CREATE switching fighters, OPTIONS pausing; and a clean console. Screenshots go to `output\pad_check`. It cannot press a real controller: the user plays with one.

## Side-View Fighters

The fighting game's fighters are seen from the side, facing each other, built in the engine beside the sandbox's front-facing body (`src/profile.rs`, `src/simulation/fighter.rs`, `src/simulation/mover.rs`; `create_side_fighters`). The [Fight](#fight) page plays them; in this repository they are played by tests and filmed, and the sandbox app keeps its front-facing bodies. Each is eleven sheets of flesh, an arm or a leg on its own layer so it passes in front of the trunk or behind it, with the sandbox's skin, fat, muscle, bone, and injury rules. A move is a pose the body is asked to take, which its muscles follow (`World::command`, `InputState::moves`): `Jab`, `Cross`, `StepIn`, `StepOut`, and, held and let go, `LeanBack`, `Duck`, and `Block`. Punches are a trained boxer's, a cross landing at about 8 m/s; a punch goes through the other fighter's open hands and lands on the head, and a block takes it on the forearms. A jab or cross thrown while ducking goes to the body instead, under the ribs, the knees staying bent and the trunk coming up out of the duck as it goes, and turns back as it lands; it passes under a block, whose forearms cover the face, and a block held while ducking, a low block, brings them down over the body, where they stop a punch to the body and let one at the head through. How they work and what they are measured against is in [`docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md`](docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md) (Punches, Knockouts).

A fighter stands on its own legs (`src/simulation/balance.rs`): the hold that puts its hips where its moves have them pushes them only from feet touching the floor, and no harder across than those feet grip, with room to spare. Each leg presses its foot down with the weight it bears, the floor takes the leg's push across through the gripping foot, and the hold's turn on the pelvis goes back on the thighs, so nothing holds the fighter up from outside. The hold bears the weight above the legs and keeps the hips over the feet where the pose puts them, steering the capture point (where the centre of mass would come to rest) away from the feet's edges and from where a stepping foot will land; the feet grip by the weight really on them, at the ends that touch the floor, each bone on the pressed flesh of its sole. A pose keeps its weight where its hips are meant to be however a duck, a lean, or a block bends the trunk (`profile::keep_the_weight`), and a lean back brings the rear heel down to bear it. A step leans the body the way it goes, hands the first foot's weight to the other, swings it along a path over the floor (carried there by its leg, `World::carry_swinging_foot`), sets it down as it touches the floor near its spot, and the other foot follows to keep the stance; one foot always stands. Shoved (`Move::Shoved`, a push on the trunk from outside, for films and tests), a fighter holds where it stands until its capture point runs past its feet, then steps to catch itself, moving its stance as far as its weight is still going; past where any step reaches (52 cm), or with its hips sunk below a quarter of its height, it has fallen: its legs stop holding it up, its joints stay as they lie, and it takes no more moves until it has got up again (below; shoved back: holds up to 40 N s, steps from 60, falls at 200). A blow that turns the head hard enough knocks a fighter out (`src/simulation/knockout.rs`): the engine watches how each fighter's head turns, substep by substep, as a boxer's mouthguard sensors do, and a fighter whose head a blow turns past what the one knockout measured on a person reached (a college football player tackled: his head's turning changed by 34 rad/s, at 12,900 rad/s²; `knockout_turn` and `knockout_spin`) goes limp, every muscle letting go, falls, and takes no more moves until it has come to and got up again (below). Punches aim at the jaw, as boxers' do: off the head's centre a blow turns it harder, and strikes to the jaw ended half of the UFC's knockouts. A cross landed well turns the struck head at about 2,800 to 3,100 rad/s², its turning changing by about 18 to 21 rad/s, about as hard as boxers' punches turn people's heads in sparring (1,520 ± 960 rad/s² on average, at most 8,850), so most punches knock no one out; one that lands on a head already turning the other way now and then does (one or two fighters in 24 random fights of 20 seconds), as one real power punch landed in a few hundred does. `--set knockout_turn=10` lets any cross do it, to see a knockout. A fighter throws no punch at one who is down or getting up, as boxing's rules have it. A duck lowers the hips no faster than legs giving way let a body fall (0.8 g): eased in at once, it asked the hips to fall at 4 g, and its folding legs pulled both feet off the floor. The settings `legs_carry` (false puts the old hold back, which pushed the hips from nowhere), `carry_strength`, `legs_across_hertz`, `legs_tilt_hertz`, and `legs_up_hertz` change it for any tool that takes `--set` or `RP_SET`.

A fighter down gets up on its own legs (`src/simulation/rise.rs`). One that fell lies until it comes to rest, 0.6 s; one knocked out lies limp for 2 s, and 15 s more for each limit's worth the blow went past the knockout limit (`knockout_seconds` and `knockout_more_seconds`), so that a blow just past the limit has a fighter up by the count of six to eight and a much harder one keeps it down past ten: knocked-out people lie limp for 2 to 16 s, and nothing measured ties how long to how hard the blow was. Its muscles then come back over 0.6 s, as BombSquad's characters' balance does once their knockout runs out. Getting up is a move like the others, a few key poses the muscles follow (`profile::sitting_up` and the rest), a stage at a time, each set on the floor where what bears the body already lies and blended into from where the body really lies, as a humanoid robot got up from its back and its front through key poses that each stood still on what touched the floor (Stückler, Schwenk and Behnke 2006). From its back a fighter sits up onto its hands, lifts its hips onto its hands and feet, and tips forward over its feet into a crouch; from its front it comes onto its hands and knees, up onto its knees, and takes a knee, its lead foot forward; out of the crouch or off the knee, it rises into its stance. The hold on its hips pushes them only from the feet or knees under them, which the floor bears as it bears a standing foot, and its hands hold its chest up, so a fighter touching the floor with nothing that could bear it cannot get up; a stage not reached within 2.5 s is begun again from where the body lies. Shoved over, a fighter is up in 5 to 6 seconds; knocked out, in 7 to 11 in random fights, and later after a much harder blow. Down or getting up, it takes no moves and the other fighter throws no punch at it, and once it stands it fights on (`World::fighter_fallen` is false again).

To film them, the app's capture mode takes `--side` and a gesture whose steps are moves: `jab`, `cross`, `step=in`, `step=out`, `back=on`, `duck=on`, `block=on` (and `=off`; a `jab` or `cross` while `duck=on` goes to the body, and `block=on` while `duck=on` blocks low), and `shove=60` (a shove of 60 N s back toward the rear foot, negative forward), the second fighter's with a 2 after the word (`cross2`, `block2=on`, `shove2=90`), with `wait=N` frames between them:

```powershell
.\target\release\realistic_physics.exe --capture output\side\frame.png --side --view normal --size 1280x720 --no-ui --no-tool --no-label --every 1 --gesture "hammer:-1.6,0.95:wait=40:step=in:wait=40:jab:wait=40:cross:wait=60:block2=on:wait=20:cross:wait=60"
```

Tools that print what they do (each an ignored test, run with `cargo test --release --lib NAME -- --ignored --nocapture`; the switches are environment variables):

- `print_fighter_punches`: a jab and a cross from 46 distances, into open hands and into a block: how often each lands and how fast, what it reddens, how far it moves the head, how hard it turns and throws the head (in rad/s² and g, as mouthguards measure boxers' heads, and how much the head's turning changes within the blow, in rad/s, as the engine judges a knockout), and whether anything tears. `RP_ONLY=cross` or `jab` and `RP_BLOCK=0` or `1` play only those, `RP_COUNTER=15` has the struck fighter step in that many frames before the punch, so it lands as a counter, `RP_DETAIL=1` prints each punch, `RP_STORY=1` what its journal kept.
- `print_random_fights`: six fights of 20 seconds with both fighters making a move about four times a second: what tore, how near the fighters stood when it did, how far each head got from where its moves put it, how hard each head was turned and whether a fighter was knocked out or fell, and the fastest a bone moved. `RP_FIGHTS=24` plays more, `RP_SECONDS=30` plays each longer, `RP_SEED=3` one fight with its story and the moves before the first thing torn, `RP_WHY=8` follows the bones it happened at through the eight frames before, phase by phase, with the limbs pressing on them, `RP_FLUNG=25` stops at the first bone moved faster than 25 m/s and traces it, `RP_POSTURE=10` prints every tenth frame how far each head is from its pose and what each fighter is doing (where its hips really are, how far its weight runs past its feet, and how each foot stands), `RP_BREAKS=1` prints what both fighters were doing whenever a bone broke, and `RP_GESTURE=1` with `RP_SEED` writes the fight as a gesture the capture mode plays, to film it (`--side --gesture`).
- `print_fighter_move`: how one fighter's joints follow a move (`RP_MOVE=jab`, `cross`, `step`, `duck`, `back`; `RP_BLOCK=1` makes it out of a block; `RP_STEPS=400` plays longer and prints where the pose puts the head and where it is). `RP_MOVES="0:duck=on,60:step=out,90:step=out"` plays a script of moves, each on its step, and prints the trunk's and the legs' joints every fifth step (`RP_EVERY=1` every step).
- `print_fighter_rest`: how a new fighter rests.
- `print_fighter_shoves`: shoves the first fighter back and then forward with growing impulses (10 to 200 N s over a tenth of a second) and prints for each whether it held, stepped, or fell, how far its hips and feet went, how low its head got, and what tore. `RP_SHOVES=40,90` picks the impulses and `RP_EVERY=5` follows each shove step by step with what the fighter's moves are doing.
- `print_fighter_support`: how hard the legs (or, with `legs_carry=false`, the old hold from nowhere) push each fighter's hips up and across and turn its pelvis, how far the hips move, and how high the head stands, through a script of moves (`RP_MOVES` as `print_fighter_move` writes it; the default steps in, takes a cross, jabs, ducks, and leans back; `RP_EVERY=10` prints every tenth step); then, for each fighter, where its centre of mass is and how fast it moves, where it and the capture point lie over the feet, how far its hips are from where the move puts them, what each foot bears and is pushed across with and what the floor gives it back, and where each foot stands. `RP_SETTLE=1` prints a fighter settling as it is built instead, `RP_SET_AFTER` changes settings once the fighters have settled, and `RP_SPACING=0.6` stands the fighters that many body heights apart. `RP_TRACE=30-34` follows the first fighter's feet and hips through every phase of those steps and prints what each phase did to them, summed over the substeps; `RP_MOMENTUM=36-40` adds up how hard each phase pushed its whole body, which shows what outside the body (the floor, gravity, drag) pushed it, since pushes within it add up to nothing.
- `print_getting_up`: knocks the second of two fighters down and prints how it lies, comes to, and gets up, stage by stage: `RP_DOWN=ko` (a cross knocks it out, the default), `back` or `front` (a shove over that way), or `flat` (a shove so hard it lands on its face); `RP_STEPS=600` steps, every `RP_EVERY=10`th: the stage it is in, where its hips are against where the stage's key pose puts them, how its feet stand, and what its feet, knees, and hands push; `RP_STAGES=1` prints why each stage begins and ends, `RP_STORY=1` what its journal kept, and `RP_MOMENTUM=480-482` how hard each phase pushed its whole body on those steps.
- `print_getting_up_in_random_fights`: random fights, `print_random_fights`'s, 40 seconds long so that a fighter going down late has time to get up: how often each fighter went down and got up, how long it was down at the longest, whether it was still down at the end, and what tore. `RP_FIGHTS=24` and `RP_SECONDS=40` as there, and `RP_SEED=9` plays one fight and prints what each fighter was doing each half second it was down.
- `print_key_poses`: how low each getting-up key pose's parts reach, to set it on the floor, and a sheet of them side by side (`output/sketch/key_poses`), for setting key poses by eye.
- `RP_SET=name=value,...` changes settings as `--set` does for `print_fighter_support`, `print_fighter_punches`, and `print_random_fights`: `RP_SET=legs_carry=false,carry_strength=0` takes every hold away, and the old fighters fall flat.

`cargo test` checks that a punch goes through open hands and a block stops it, that a punch to the body goes under a block and a low block stops it while a punch at the head goes over it, that body punches and low blocks thrown and held as a player would keep a fighter on its feet, that fighters step no nearer than they stand and stay mirror images stepping in together, that a ducking fighter stepping back keeps its pose, that two fighters ducking toward each other sit back, that a fighter shoved over backward or forward gets up within seven seconds with both feet on the floor and nothing torn, and that one knocked out lies limp, takes no moves, and then gets up into its stance while the other throws no punch at it; `random_fights_leave_the_fighters_whole` (ignored; `verify.ps1 -Deep` runs it) plays the six random fights and fails on a torn joint, a broken bone, a bone flung, or a fighter out of its pose.

## Controls

- Left-drag to swing the selected tool into the body. The tool moves with your pointer like a tool held firmly in hand: swing from outside the body for a hard hit, or hold the tool against the body and keep dragging past it to press harder. The ring marks where your hand is. Damage comes from the tool's shape and weight, its speed when it lands, and how hard you press; it grows steeply with speed, so slow moves only bruise.
- `H`, `B`, `S`, and `F` select the sledgehammer (heavy), bat (blunt), knife (sharp), and bare fist. The sledgehammer is in hand when the app starts.
- `←`, or the LEFT punch button, and `→`, or RIGHT, make the body throw a straight punch with its arm on that side, out to that side, and back into its guard. An arm left limp by a broken bone throws none.
- The app has one body, standing where the scenarios strike it and drawn as large as fits, to use as a punching bag; two fighters box on the [Fight](#fight) page.
- `D` toggles the contact debug overlay.
- `R` resets the fighters, or the body.
- `Space` pauses or resumes.
- `M`, or the speaker button, turns the sound off and on.
- The start screen's Fight button opens the [Fight](#fight) page, the fighting game built on the engine.
- `I`, or the about button, tells what the simulation is and who made it, with links to how it was made, the video, and the source code. In the browser it opens the start screen again as an about card over the paused simulation; the button on the card, `Escape`, or a click beside the card goes back, and the simulation runs again unless it was paused before. The desktop app opens the write-up in your browser instead.
- The control chips along the bottom are also buttons: click or tap them for the same actions.
- On touch screens, drag a finger to strike. The chips grow to finger size after the first touch. A finger put down somewhere new takes the tool up there rather than flinging it across the screen, and a tool taken up on the body passes through it until clear.

## Compare A Change

To see how a change moves the simulation, compare the working tree with a commit (by default `HEAD`, so it shows what uncommitted changes do):

```powershell
.\tools\compare.ps1
.\tools\compare.ps1 -Ref HEAD~3 -Sweep
.\tools\compare.ps1 -Capture torso_heavy_high
```

It plays the strike scenarios and the visual damage and anatomy diagnostics on both sides and lists every changed number per scenario, tuning warnings that appeared or went away, and changed mesh counts. The commit is built in a git worktree under `target\compare` and its results are cached by commit, so repeated comparisons only replay the working tree. `-Sweep` also compares how often each scenario stays in band when its swing moves slightly, `-Fights N` and `-Random N` play the same random fights and random blows on both sides and set them side by side (see [Look Into A Run](#look-into-a-run)), and `-Capture NAME` puts app screenshots of that scenario from both sides into one contact sheet; each needs a commit that already has those features. To run every check a change must pass before it merges, with one verdict, see [Check A Change Before Merging](#check-a-change-before-merging). The full report is `output\compare\report.txt`, with each side's raw outputs in `output\compare\base` and `output\compare\current`. `-Clean` removes the cached worktrees and results.

To see how a change alters what the body does under a blow, film the same swing on a commit and on the working tree:

```powershell
.\tools\before_after.ps1 -Scenario bat_hard_forearm -Every 12 -Frames 72 -Crop 400,30,480,590
.\tools\before_after.ps1 -Strike "bat:-0.4,-0.24:0.1,0.16:frames=9:settle=90" -Every 16 -Frames 96 -Name head
.\tools\before_after.ps1 -Scenario punch_to_the_head -RefScenario bat_to_the_head -Every 1 -Frames 106 -Steps 12,15,16,18,21,26,40,106 -Crop 330,20,620,440
```

It plays the swing in the real app with the commit's build (`-Ref`, by default `main`, built and kept as `bench.ps1` keeps it) and with the working tree's, saving the screen every `-Every` steps, and lays the two film strips out in one sheet, the commit's frames above the working tree's, so each column shows the same moment (`output\before_after\NAME-before-after.png`). A scenario plays on both sides only if the commit has it; `-RefScenario` plays another on the commit's side, `-Strike` or `-Gesture` plays the same swing on both, and `-RefGesture` another gesture on the commit's side, as when the working tree's uses a punch or a tool the commit lacks. `-Steps` shows only the steps listed, such as the few around a blow from a strip saved every step. `-Crop X,Y,W,H` shows just that part of each frame, such as the body, `-View anatomy` the see-through view, and `-NoBuild` uses the working tree's last build. A strip with an empty frame, which the app saves while the display sleeps, stops the sheet.

To show how something moves, which a sheet of stills hides, turn a film strip into a short film (an animated GIF):

```powershell
python tools\film.py output\films\punch.gif output\captures\fighter_punches_the_guard-1280x720-*.png --crop 370,40,600,600 --width 480 --ms 67 --mark 439,632
python tools\film.py output\films\crossing.gif output\captures\before-1280x720-*.png --below output\captures\after-1280x720-*.png --label "before" --below-label "after"
```

It plays the frames in order (a glob is expanded in order), `--crop` cuts each to that rectangle, `--ms` is how long each frame shows (67 plays frames saved every 2 steps at half speed), `--width` scales it, `--label` writes a caption, `--mark X,Y` draws a small white tick at that pixel of each frame, such as where a foot stood, and `--below` plays a second strip under the first, frame by frame, for a before-and-after film. It needs Pillow (`python -m pip install pillow`).

## Check A Change Before Merging

A change is ready to merge when what it set out to do works and nothing that must hold is clearly worse than on `main`. One command runs every check that decides it, on the working tree and on a commit (`main` unless `-Ref`), and prints one verdict:

```powershell
.\tools\merge_check.ps1
.\tools\merge_check.ps1 -Ref v0.15.1 -Fight ..\realistic_physics_fight
```

What must hold: the tests and `verify.ps1`; the browser build simulating as the native one (`parity.ps1`) and still running smoothly on a phone (`touch_check.ps1`, upright and sideways); and, no worse beyond chance, the scenarios' bands and real-life checks across the sweep, the rules every play follows in random blows, and the watchdogs for a body that breaks (a place that is not a number, broken bone gone from the body, a whole bone outside the skin, a whole joint pulled apart, blood that does not add up) in the sweep and in 2000 random blows from each of three seeds. Clearly worse means a significance test flags it (`*`: chance makes a difference that large under 5% of the time, `**` under 1%), and a real-life check a few runs could flip is reported as borderline, not held. The rest may drift and is reported: the other watchdogs, struck flesh faster than twice the tool, the injuries random blows do, the sweep's medians, random fights set fight against fight, the moved-body tests, and the step time against the commit.

It runs `verify.ps1` (without the sweep), `compare.ps1 -Sweep -Fights 300 -SideFights 100 -Random 2000` against the commit, the moved-body tests, `parity.ps1`, `bench.ps1`, and the phone checks, each logged in `output\merge_check`, about 40 minutes in all; the commit's side is cached for the next round against it. A step that passed on the same working tree is not run again, so a round stopped partway picks up where it stopped (`-Fresh` runs every step), and `-Skip bench,web` leaves steps out, their checks listed as not run. `-Fight PATH` also builds the site with the game's Fight page and plays its buttons on a phone. The verdict is `output\merge_check\report.txt`: each check `passed`, `same`, `better`, `WORSE`, `FAILED`, or `LOOK` (for a person to judge, such as a phone check drawing under 60 frames a second), with what changed under it, and the script exits 1 when a check that must hold failed or got clearly worse. With many things compared, a lone `*` may be chance: play it again with other seeds (`-Seeds 4,5,6`) before chasing it. Every comparison writes what it compared to a findings file beside its report (`sweep_diff.csv`, `random_diff.csv`, `fights_diff.csv`, `side_fights_diff.csv`), which the verdict reads. In the side-view fights, each fighter's torn and slipped joints, broken bones, and falls with no knockout must hold; a commit without `--side-fights` (before v0.18) is listed as not compared on them, which does not block.

## Check Browser Parity

The browser build simulates exactly like the desktop app, to the last bit, so scenarios tuned natively hold on the website. To check it after a change:

```powershell
.\tools\parity.ps1
```

It builds the native app and the web version, runs `realistic_physics.exe --selftest`, plays `?selftest` in headless Edge (or Chrome), and lists every scenario whose injuries, blood loss, tool turn, or tissue speed differ between the two, with each build's step time. It exits 1 on any difference and writes both reports and `report.txt` to `output\parity`. `-SkipBuild` reuses the last builds, `-Port` changes the local port the page is served on (8090), and `-Browser` takes another Chromium browser.

Float arithmetic and square roots come out the same in both builds, but the standard library's trigonometry, `hypot`, `exp`, `ln`, and `powf` do not: natively they call the platform's C runtime (Microsoft's on Windows), in WebAssembly a port of musl's libm, and the two disagree in the last bit on many inputs. So the simulation calls the [`libm`](https://crates.io/crates/libm) crate, that same port, instead, and the `library_math_is_the_same_in_every_build` test fails on any standard-library call of those in code the simulation runs.

To check touch input on a phone without a phone:

```powershell
.\tools\touch_check.ps1
.\tools\touch_check.ps1 -Size 844x390 -SkipBuild
```

It builds the web version (unless `-SkipBuild`) and plays it in headless Edge (or Chrome) as a phone with a touch screen (390x844 by default), with touches sent the way a finger's arrive: a first touch on the chest, which grows the buttons to finger size and must take the hammer up without striking the body, then a firm swipe into the belly, which must strike it (the page counts the blow), all with no errors logged. It saves a screenshot after each in `output\touch_check` and exits 1 on a failure. The headless browser keeps drawing frames at full rate, which the app's browser pane does not while it is hidden.

## Screenshots

The app can play a scripted strike and save the screen without anyone at the keyboard:

```powershell
.\tools\capture.ps1
.\tools\capture.ps1 -Scenario torso_heavy_high -Sizes 1280x720,800x600,390x844
.\tools\capture.ps1 -Strike "hammer:-0.26,0.34:0.30,0.34:frames=14" -NoUi
.\tools\capture.ps1 -Gesture "bat:-0.3,0.3:wait=10:down:-0.08,0.3/15:-0.08,0.6/60:up:wait=20" -Every 5 -View normal
.\tools\capture.ps1 -Fighters -Gesture "hammer:-1.3,0.95:wait=10:punch2=right:wait=60" -Every 2 -NoTool
```

Each capture briefly opens the app window at the given size, plays the strike at fixed steps, saves the screen as PNG files in `output\captures`, and closes; several captures are also laid out in one contact sheet (`NAME-sheet.png`). A window only changes how large the body is drawn, never what happens to it, so with several sizes the script also checks that each played the same: each capture writes its step-by-step log (`NAME-SIZE-events.csv`) and prints a digest of the world it left, and the script fails, naming the first step that differs, when they are not all the same. Besides the view players see, a capture can draw an anatomy view with see-through skin that shows the muscle, vessels, and bones; it exists only for checking, and the app itself always shows the one normal view. `-View normal|anatomy|both` picks the views (both by default), `-Frames N` stops after N steps, `-Every N` also saves the screen every N steps as a film strip of how things move (laid out in the sheet), `-NoUi` and `-NoLabel` leave out the HUD and control buttons and the name and step label, for clean images, and `-NoTool` leaves out the tool, to see what lies under it. `-Variant ALONG,ACROSS,STRENGTH` plays a scenario as one run of the sweep does (`strike_scenarios --variant`), to look at a run the sweep found out of band. `-Set NAME=VALUE` plays it with an engine setting changed, as `strike_scenarios --set` does (see [Look Into A Run](#look-into-a-run)), to film what a setting does. `-Gesture` plays a gesture instead of a swing (see [Strike Scenarios](#strike-scenarios)). A capture plays the scenarios' lone body, drawn as the app draws its one body, a fight scenario its two fighters as the scenarios stand them, and with no play at all the app as it starts; `-Fighters` plays any swing or gesture on two fighters side by side, as the fight scenarios stand them (`-Spacing S` stands them S body heights apart), and a gesture's `punch2=left` or `punch2=right` throws the second fighter's punches (its arm nearer the first is its `right`, as the first's mirror image). The script drives `realistic_physics.exe --capture OUT.png` (options `--size`, `--view`, `--scenario`, `--strike`, `--gesture`, `--variant`, `--set`, `--frames`, `--every`, `--no-ui`, `--no-tool`, `--label`, `--no-label`, `--body-frame`, `--events`, `--sound`, `--fighters`, `--spacing`), and `contact_sheet.exe OUT.png [--height H] [--columns N] [--crop X,Y,W,H] IN.png...` makes the sheet (`--crop` shows just that part of each image).

`.\tools\see_through.ps1 [-Scenarios NAME,...] [-NoBuild]` checks that no wound lets the background show through the body: it captures the end of every tuned scenario and the rest pose without the HUD, labels, or tool (in `output\see_through`), and `see_through.exe` counts the patches of background each body encloses, with their size and place, exiting 1 if it finds any. A gap between body parts that a limb or a piece of bone closes off counts too, so look at a capture before calling its hole a wound's.

Captures can also become videos: `--every 1 --no-ui --no-label` saves every 60 Hz step as a clean frame. `--body-frame X,Y,H` draws the top of the head at `X,Y` and the body `H` pixels tall instead of fitting it to the window, so `--size 576x1024 --body-frame 288,240,561.6` films a vertical video with room above the head for captions. It only places the drawing: the body is simulated where the scenarios play it in any window, so every video's swings do what the scenarios check. `--events FILE` writes what happens at each step as CSV (the tool, its speed and impact, whether it touches the body, and running counts of broken bones, torn skin and muscle, bruises, and blood drops), so a video's sound can be made from the same run as its picture. `--sound FILE` writes the game's own sound for the run instead: a WAV mixed step by step as the app plays it, the same mix as `strike_scenarios --sound` (byte for byte, in any window), so a video can carry the sound the game makes.

## Measure Speed

To see whether a change makes the simulation faster or slower, time it against a commit:

```powershell
.\tools\bench.ps1 -Ref main
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --phases
```

`bench.ps1` plays the app's native self-test with the working tree's release build and the commit's (built in the same cached worktree as `compare.ps1`, with that commit's own release profile), taking turns for `-Runs` rounds (default 5) so changes in the machine's load hit both alike. It reports each build's best and median average step and slowest step, the speedup, and whether every scenario's injuries stayed the same, as they must when a change only makes the simulation faster (`output\bench\report.txt`). `-BaseExe` and `-Exe` time two prebuilt apps instead. Builds or other heavy work on the machine show up as noise, so compare the best runs and add runs when the two are close.

`strike_scenarios --phases` times each phase of a step (springs, areas, the contact passes, the tool's sweep, and so on) over the tuned scenarios, or `--only` some of them, or a custom `--strike` or `--gesture`, on one thread, and prints where the time goes, slowest phase first, with each phase's worst step and each scenario's average and slowest step (`output\strike_phases.txt`). The world times its phases only when given a clock (`World::time_phases`), which the app never does.

## Strike Scenarios

`.\tools\verify.ps1` builds and runs deterministic strike playback across representative torso, shoulder, arm, hip, and leg strikes with blunt, sharp, and heavy tools, plus gestures that check how steadily a tool moves in hand, punches with the bare fist, the figure's own punches, and fights, where one of two fighters punches the other. The scenarios live in `src/scenarios.rs`, where the strike runner, the visual damage diagnostic, and the app's capture mode all play them from. Each scripted swing moves the hand from outside the body and holds briefly at the end with the button down, so the tool, which trails the hand, lands as a real swing would. A gesture instead plays the tool the way the app does: the tool is in hand from the start and hovers with the button up, and the hand moves, presses, holds, and lets go on cue. The scenario target writes frame-by-frame contact telemetry to:

```text
output\strike_scenarios.csv
```

It also writes a compact per-scenario tuning summary to:

```text
output\strike_summary.csv
```

It also writes a warning-only tuning report that compares each scenario against what its play does in real life and against expected steadiness bands:

```text
output\strike_tuning_report.txt
```

The CSV outputs include region, intent, tool mode, striker speed, impact, contact counts, contact depth, tissue/bone loads, sharp cut propagation counts, sharp skin-flap delamination counts, fiber-aligned muscle tear counts, muscle crush-rupture counts, torso cavity pressure/collapse/rupture counts, organ damage/direct-penetration/rib-puncture/rupture counts, direct and fragment-driven major vessel laceration counts, soft-tissue contusion counts, local tissue-softening maxima, spring-fatigue events and local fatigue maxima, plastic deformation events and local plasticity maxima, joint subluxation/breakage, ligament/capsule damage events, fracture events, rib-fracture counts, fracture marrow-source counts, post-fracture joint limit corrections, wound counts, wound reopen counts, wound pressure/clotting, blood loss, final blood reserve, final blood-turgor scale, blood stain/pool deposits, blood that welled onto the skin with its drips and how far it ran, broken-end tissue contacts, inside-out skin puncture counts, fragment-bone contacts/damping/resting support, fragment-pair contacts/damping/resting support, fragment-floor contacts/resting support, overlap depth, fragment angular speed, free/spinning/sleeping fragment counts, runtime budget checks/skips/replacements, fluid emission, final fragment counts, and accumulated damage stats. Each scenario states what its play does to a real person and how often, with the research behind it, in place of injury counts tuned to the engine; [docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md](docs/INJURY_REFERENCE.md) has the numbers and the sources. The figure stands for a 1.75 m adult, so speeds, energies, and blood are compared in real units: a hard bat swing reaches about 25 m/s at the barrel, a full-force sledgehammer about 10.6 m/s (3400 px/s), and a fast knife about 15 m/s. How often a blow breaks a bone follows its energy against what breaks that bone: a hard bat swing into the forearm raised in the guard always breaks it and drops the arm out of the guard, and a moderate one sometimes breaks it, a full-force sledgehammer swing through the guard's elbow into the chest usually breaks the upper arm and sometimes a rib, a medium bat blow to the chest only bruises, a bat to the head bruises it and rarely breaks the skull, and a light tap on the guard knocks the fist aside without breaking anything, after which the arm brings it back into the guard; a bat's breaks rarely come out through the skin, a sledgehammer's sometimes. A knife always opens the skin, but a slash on the belly rarely reaches an artery. Four rules hold for every play: bleeding follows what was cut (at most 10 ml a minute from each wound that misses arteries, at most 50 from an open fracture's marrow, and at least 10 from a cut artery), a knife never breaks a long bone, broken bone stays in the body (no piece ends up more than three point spacings from all flesh, `bone_pieces_out`), and struck flesh moves at most twice as fast as the tool that hit it (or the figure's fist, when it punches). A check the engine cannot meet yet is listed as a known gap, with why, and reported apart; one is open now, a punch on the guard that drops the arm (below). The sweep also reports how often a piece of a closed break, or a whole bone, ends up outside the skin, where neither belongs (`closed_pieces_out`, `whole_bones_out`): a hard blow can still squeeze the flat body's flesh off a broken bone. Bands also hold the engine to how it should work: knife cuts across the belly and down the inside of the arm must open rather than leave a line of torn skin; the belly cut, which misses the big vessels, must show its blood welling out, and the arm cut's artery must bleed out of the cut and run down the arm; `thigh_cut_rebleed` lets a knife cut down the thigh bleed down the leg and clot and then requires a bat blow to make it bleed again; and `torso_heavy_fragment_settle` waits after the sledgehammer blow so fragment contact, resting support, and sleep behavior are covered. Every scenario also checks that the tool never snaps round more than 30 degrees in one step. The gesture scenarios hold a bat or hammer to more: `bat_carried_steady` carries the bat across the body and back with the button up, where it must stay upright; `bat_drag_steady` and `hammer_drag_steady` press the tool against the side of the chest and drag it down the body, and `bat_swing_back_steady` swings the bat across the chest and back, where the tool may turn at most 16 degrees a step and 45 degrees in all (50 for the swing across the chest and back, which bounces off the arm). A speed ladder holds damage to the speed of the blow: `hammer_slow_push`, `hammer_moderate_swing`, and `hammer_firm_swing` push the sledgehammer into the side of the body at about 400, 800, and 1600 px/s, where a slow push must do no harm, a moderate swing only bruise, and a firm one bruise and usually break the forearm raised in the guard, as a firm swing can, and `bat_firm_swing` must bruise and rarely break bone. Punches: `punch_to_the_head`, `punch_on_the_guard`, and `punch_to_the_ribs` drive the bare fist at about 8 m/s, a hard punch, into the side of the head above the guard's fist, the forearm raised in the guard, and the lowest ribs under the guard's elbow, and straight back out, as a punch is pulled back; each must usually bruise and rarely split the skin, the head must be knocked aside on the neck, and the skull and the forearm never break and a rib only rarely, as in 141 bare-knuckle bouts (Muzzi et al. 2022). A punch on the guard tears the elbow and drops the arm in more than half the runs, as the moderate bat swing's blow on it does: a known gap. `throws_a_punch_right` and `throws_a_punch_left` have the figure throw a punch into the air with each arm, which must be as fast as an untrained man's hardest punch (5.7 to 11.1 m/s, Adamec et al. 2021), touch and hurt nothing, and come back into the guard. Fights stand two fighters side by side and have the left punch the right (`Fight` sets how far apart they stand, whether the struck fighter's near guard drops first, and how much lower the punch aims; the sweep moves how far apart they stand and where the punch aims rather than a swing): `fighter_punches_the_head`, its near guard dropped, and `fighter_punches_the_guard`, its guard up, which the punching arm rides over, the guard's fist standing beside the chin. Each must usually bruise the struck fighter and rarely split its skin, and never break its skull or forearm nor drop its guard, and the puncher's own hand must never break, as in bare-knuckle bouts (8 hand fractures among 282 fighters over whole bouts, Muzzi et al. 2022; a metacarpal bends to failure at about 1150 N, Pianta et al. 2013); the struck fighter's breaks and torn skin are judged apart from the puncher's (`PuncherBreaks`). A punch at the ribs was tried and left out: in the front view the struck fighter's own arm covers the side of its chest, raised or hanging, and the punch broke its collarbone instead. `hammer_finger_lands` and `hammer_first_touch` play the way a phone is used: a finger put down on the chest, far from where the hammer rested or on a body nothing has touched yet, must do no harm at all. `hammer_into_wound` smashes a wound open with four full-force blows, then drags the sledgehammer slowly into it and holds it there, where it must end up close to the hand: flesh torn away must not hold a tool back, as it once held it 33 px behind. It ends a few pixels behind in most runs; where the blows left the arm's outer flesh whole the hammer rests on it, and where the arm keeps its guard the elbow it smashed stops the hammer short of the hand, up to about 57 px out, so the band allows 60.

A strike's outcome can swing a lot with a few pixels of aim, because a fast tool moves about one mesh spacing per step. So the verifier also replays every scenario with its swing moved by up to 0.01 body heights along its path and 0.005 across it (gestures move 0.01 across the body and 0.005 down it), on bones 20% weaker, as tuned, and 25% stronger, since real bones differ that much between people (45 runs each, on all cores). It writes how often each one stays in band, how often each injury happened against how often it happens in real life, and the spread of its main injuries and of how steady the tool was, to:

```text
output\strike_sweep_report.txt
output\strike_sweep.csv
```

Every run of the sweep, the moved-body tests, and the random blows starts from the same body for its window and settings, built and settled under its weight, so the scenario tool builds each such body once and plays every run on a copy of it (`src/bin/strike_scenarios/bodies.rs`): the results are exactly what a body built afresh gives (`a_copied_body_plays_as_one_built_afresh`), and the sweep and the random blows take about half the time.

The sweep report ends with what the watchdogs flagged in each scenario's runs ([Look Into A Run](#look-into-a-run)). `python tools\sweep_summary.py [REPORT...]` sums a sweep report up in a few lines (runs in band, real-life mismatches, closed breaks' pieces and whole bones outside the skin, bone thrown out, and the scenarios behind each), to compare two sweeps at a glance.

The runner also takes options for quick experiments:

```powershell
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --list
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only torso_heavy_high --sweep
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --strike "knife:-0.08,0.38:0.07,0.48"
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --gesture "bat:-0.35,0.33:wait=10:down:0,0.33/12:wait=60:up:wait=30"
```

`--list` prints every scenario's swing, `--only` limits a run or sweep to some scenarios, `--variant ALONG,ACROSS,STRENGTH` plays them as one run of the sweep does (the first columns of `strike_sweep.csv`), to look into a run the sweep found out of band, `--fastest SPEED` also prints each frame on which some tissue moved faster than SPEED px/s (which point, its layer and weight, and what holds it), `--trace point:N[:FROM-TO]` or `--trace bone:N[:FROM-TO]` prints what each phase of each step did to that point or bone, substep by substep (how far it moved it and how it changed its speed), to find what throws it, `--stance N` prints every N frames how the body holds its stance (each joint's turn off the guard and its muscles' torque, how far a punching arm's muscles aim each of its joints past the guard, how far the hips and fists are from where they stood, each body's hips' shift and tilt and each foot's rise off the floor and slide along it, how far the flesh hangs off its place on the bones, the bones farthest from where they were built, and the fastest tissue), `--between N` prints every N frames how two fighters' limbs press on each other (how many places and how hard, how deep one's skin pressed into the other's, and the bones of different fighters nearer than the skin both carry at rest, closest first), `--journal` and `--why` say what happened and what moved a thing ([Look Into A Run](#look-into-a-run)), and one scenario played on its own (`--only NAME`, or with `--variant`) also lists where its broken bone ended up: each piece, whether it lies inside the skin, and what holds it, and for a closed break's piece outside the skin the flesh that keeps its place on it and how far that flesh lies from its place. And `--strike TOOL:U0,V0:U1,V1[:power=P][:frames=N][:windup=N][:settle=N]` plays one custom swing in body coordinates (fractions of body height from the top of the head on the midline) and prints its injuries, or their spread with `--sweep`. Tools are `bat`, `knife`, `hammer`, and `fist`, and each swings with its strength in the app unless `power=P` tries another. `--gesture TOOL:U,V[:STEP...]` plays a gesture the way the app does: the hand starts at `U,V`, and the steps are `U,V/N` to move there over N steps (`U,V` jumps), `down` and `up` for the button, `punch=left` and `punch=right` for the figure's punches, `lean=on` and `lean=off` for its lean back, and `wait=N`. `fighters` (or `fighters=S`, S body heights apart; `--fighters [--spacing S]` adds it) stands two fighters for it as the fight scenarios do, where `punch2=` and `lean2=` work the second fighter, `guard=down` drops the struck fighter's near guard first, and `aim=A` aims the punches A radians higher; `--list` writes the fights this way, so a fight's play pasted into `--gesture` plays as the scenario does. A custom swing or gesture prints its injuries, how the skin opened (fibers a blade cut, fibers torn, and the stretches of cut or tear that opened), for a knife how well its cut follows the blade (the blade's path on the steps it cut fibers, the slit's length in the rest shape against it, how far the slit runs from it, and how wide the cut gapes in skin and muscle), and how steady the tool was (its largest turn in one step, its turning in all, snaps, how often a held tool lost and found the body again, how far it trailed the hand in the air and at the end, and the fastest tissue), and writes its frame-by-frame telemetry, including the tool's angle and position and what its striking part reaches (points of flesh, points of flesh torn away, bones, and the spine), to `output\strike_custom_frames.csv`.

## Look Into A Run

To watch a run, open it in the viewer:

```powershell
.\tools\inspect.ps1 -Scenario bat_hard_forearm
.\tools\inspect.ps1 -Strike "knife:0.331,0.406:-0.299,0.447:power=3:frames=8:windup=6:settle=87" -Frame 9
.\tools\inspect.ps1 -Scenario bat_hard_forearm -Frame 11 -Overlays bones,events -Select bone:14 -Why -Shot output\inspect\forearm.png
.\tools\inspect.ps1 -Fighters -Gesture "hammer:-1.3,0.95:wait=10:punch2=right:wait=4:punch=right:wait=40" -Frame 27 -Overlays bones
```

It plays the scenario, swing, or gesture once (`-Variant` for one run of the sweep; `-Fighters [-Spacing S]` plays a gesture on two fighters), then steps through it: Space plays and pauses, Left and Right step a frame (ten with Shift), Home and End jump to the ends, and a click on the timeline along the bottom jumps there; the timeline has a tick for each frame something was hurt and a red mark where a watchdog first flagged something. The panel on the right tells what the injury rules decided on the frame and what the watchdogs flag on it. Keys draw over the body: 1 the skin's stretch, blue where squeezed and yellow to red toward tearing (again for the muscle's), 2 bruising and crushed flesh, 3 what moves and how fast, 4 where each injury happened (on at the start), 5 the bones, numbered, and the joints with how far each is turned; A shows the see-through view. Click a point of flesh or a bone to select it: the panel says what it is, how it moves, how far it is from its place, and what happened to it, and W adds what moved it over the last 20 frames (the why, below); the mouse wheel scrolls the panel. `-Shot FILE` saves the frame as it would show, with `-Overlays`, `-Select`, and `-Why`, and exits, to look at a run without anyone at the keyboard. The script drives `realistic_physics.exe --inspect` (options `--scenario`, `--strike`, `--gesture`, `--variant`, `--fighters`, `--spacing`, `--frame`, `--overlays`, `--select`, `--why`, `--shot`).

When a run does something unexpected, these say what happened and why, so a fix can start from the cause rather than from guesses:

```powershell
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only bat_hard_forearm --journal
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only bat_hard_forearm --why triangle:91:0-13
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only torso_heavy_fragment_settle
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --moved
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only shoulder_blunt --twin mirrored
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --random 2000 --seed 7
```

- **The journal** (`--journal`) lists every injury the rules decided, frame by frame: what tore, broke, came loose, or bled, where on the body, what did it (the tool, a broken piece of bone, the muscle pressed onto a bone, pressure in the chest), the measure that crossed the rule's limit (such as "stretched +69% (limit +60%)" or "2.32 times its strength"), and the phase of the step that decided it; then the totals. It also tells when a punch turned back, a fist landing or an arm pressed too hard by the other fighter's ("met at 8.1 m/s", "pressed 402 N"). Left and right are as seen on screen, each from the body's own middle; with two fighters each part is named by its fighter ("the right fighter's left forearm"). The world keeps a journal only when asked (`World::keep_journal`), and keeping one changes nothing it does (`tests\inspection_tests.rs`).
- **The why** (`--why point:N`, `bone:N`, `spring:N`, `joint:N`, or `triangle:N`, then `:FROM-TO` frames) follows a point, a bone (its middle and its angle), a fiber (its ends moving apart), a joint (its bend, and how far its bones' ends were pulled apart, with each phase's share of that), or a triangle (a corner crossing the side across from it) through every phase of every step, and splits its motion into each phase's share: a phase that changed its velocity by dv at time t moved it dv times the time left, so the shares and the motion it already had add up to how far it went. It plays the same frames with nothing touching the body as well and takes those shares out, so forces that only hold the body at rest, such as the skin outline's hold against the skin's pull, cancel and what the blow changed is left, and it names the frames that moved the thing most.
- **The watchdogs** watch every frame of every scenario and sweep run for what makes no physical sense: a point or bone not at a finite place; tissue or bone thrown faster than twice the fastest the tool or a punching fist went (and 1000 px/s); a piece of broken bone leaving the body, and a closed break's piece or a whole bone outside the skin; a whole joint whose bone's end has moved more than a point spacing off its place, pulled open or pushed aside; living flesh turned inside out by a quarter of its area or more; tissue still moving faster than 40 px/s, or an unhurt arm out of its guard, once nothing has touched the body for 1.5 s and no body is punching; and blood that does not add up. A scenario played on its own (`--only`, `--variant`, `--strike`, `--gesture`) prints what they flagged and, for the first thing each flagged, the why over the 20 frames before; the sweep report lists for each scenario how many runs each watchdog tripped in, with a run to replay (`--variant`) and the thing to follow (`--why`). They report and do not judge: a run they flag still passes.
- **The moved-body tests** (`--moved`, about seven minutes on all cores) replay each scenario's sweep, all but the fights, which already play two bodies, with the body mirrored left to right, and standing where each of two fighters stands in the same world (`rp::fighter_frames`), and compare each run with its match as tuned: how often each real-life outcome happened and how often the runs stayed in band, by Fisher's exact test, and the medians of the main injuries, by the sign test, each difference marked by how unlikely chance is to make it. The same sweep aimed a millionth of a pixel off is judged alongside: it differs by chance alone, so it shows how many differences chance gives. A body standing elsewhere is the scenarios' body moved there (`World::move_across`), as a second fighter is. Then a body falls, and another drifts at 1 m/s with no gravity, free of everything for a second, which should hurt nothing and leave its flesh in place on its bones. A difference means a rule reads where something is in the world, is not the same on the body's two sides, or judges motion against the world rather than against the flesh around it (`output\strike_moved_report.txt`). `--mirrored` plays any scenario mirrored, to look into one. Bodies of other sizes are not played: every body is simulated at the scenarios' size and only drawn larger or smaller, which `capture.ps1` checks across window sizes.
- **Twin runs** (`--twin mirrored`, or `--twin across=PX`, with `--only`; not for fights) play a scenario beside a twin of the body mirrored left to right or moved PX pixels across (208 by default), step by step, and say why the two part where the moved-body tests find they differ. The report says how far apart the twins stand at rest and whether they were built differently (fibers, fat ties, or muscle ties without a twin, matching fibers resting at different lengths, the outline holding matching points differently), then frame by frame how far apart the tool, the worst point, and the worst bone have come, by how far each moved since the play began, beside a third copy with the hand nudged a millionth of a pixel, which parts on rounding alone. On the first frame they part beyond rounding, or on `--twin-frame N`, it lists phase by phase where they moved things differently, beyond what holds each at rest: the rule that is lopsided or reads where the body stands. It found the knife taken up facing one way, a bat brought straight down turning its handle to one side, fat ties and long fibers chosen by the order the body was built in, a settle that leaned the body, and contacts resolved in the order they lay across the world.
- **Random fights** (`--fights N`, with `--seed S`) plays N short fights as the Fight page plays them: the left fighter taps punch and lean back at random, a press every 2 to 24 steps for 5 s, as a player hammering the buttons does, while the right one punches every 1.5 to 3.5 s, as the sparring partner does. The report counts each fighter's joints torn apart, and which joints, its joints slipped out of place, its broken bones and torn skin, and what the watchdogs flagged, each fight written in the syntax `--gesture` reads so it replays exactly (`output\random_fights_report.txt`, and every fight in `output\random_fights.csv`). The same seed plays the same fights. `--mix spam` leaves out the leans and the right fighter's punches, and `--mix spam-lean` the right fighter's punches, the left one's punches landing on the same steps in every mix, to tell which press does a harm. Two sets of fights go side by side with `--diff A B` (folders holding `random_fights.csv`; `output\fights_diff.txt`): each fighter's torn and slipped joints, joint by joint, broken bones, torn skin, and the fights each watchdog tripped in, A -> B, each difference marked by how unlikely chance is to make it. When both sides played the same fights (the same count, seed, and mix), each fight is set against itself, by the sign test on the fights that came out differently, so a hundred fights tell a real change from chance better than counts over several seeds read by eye, and the fights each side came out of more hurt are listed to replay; sides that played different fights are compared as two samples (Fisher's exact test). `.\tools\compare.ps1 -Fights 100` plays the same fights on a commit and on the working tree and sets them side by side, and `.\tools\ab.ps1 -Fights 100` two ways in one build; a hundred fights take about a minute.
- **Random side-view fights** (`--side-fights N`, with `--seed S`) plays N fights between the [side-view fighters](#side-view-fighters), seeds S to S+N-1, 20 s each, both fighters making a move about four times a second, drawn at random from jab, cross, step in and out, and lean back, duck, and block held or let go: the engine's own random fights (`rp::play_side_fight`, `src/simulation/side_fights.rs`), so fight 15 here is fight 15 of the `print_random_fights` test, step for step, and can be followed there with `RP_SEED`, `RP_WHY`, and `RP_GESTURE`. The report counts each fighter's joints torn apart and put out, and which, its broken bones and torn skin, knockouts, falls with no knockout, how often it went down and got up and its longest time down, and how far its head strayed from its pose (`output\side_fights_report.txt`, every fight in `output\side_fights.csv`). Two sets go side by side with `--diff A B` (`output\side_fights_diff.txt`), fight against fight when both played the same seeds, each difference marked by how sure it is, and the fights each side came out of more hurt listed with their seeds. `.\tools\compare.ps1 -SideFights 100` plays the same fights on a commit and on the working tree; a hundred take about four minutes a side. Use it for any change to the side-view fighters, their moves, or what their blows do, where 24 fights read by eye against figures in the notes could not tell a change from chance.
- **Random testing** (`--random N`, with `--seed S` and `--tools bat,hammer,knife`, the tools it throws unless told, or `fist` among them) plays N random blows, a few hundred a minute on all cores: any tool, through any point of skin, from any side, at any speed, or pressed in, held, and dragged. Each is watched by the watchdogs and judged by the rules every play follows, and the report lists each rule broken and each watchdog tripped, with how many plays and plays to replay: every play is written in the syntax `--strike` and `--gesture` read, rounded as written, so any of them replays exactly, with `--journal` and `--why` to follow (`output\random_blows_report.txt`, and every play with what it did in `output\random_blows.csv`). The same seed throws the same plays. With `--shrink`, the first play to break each rule or trip each watchdog is shrunk to the simplest play like it that still does: aimed at the same point of skin with the same tool, but as slow, short, and level as it can be and without a drag, by proptest's binary search on each of the numbers the play is drawn from. The simplest blow that still goes wrong is the easiest to follow. Two sets of blows go side by side with `--diff A B` (folders holding `random_blows.csv`, or folders of such folders, a seed each, pooled; `output\random_diff.txt`): the plays each watchdog tripped in and each rule was broken in, tool by tool, and the plays that broke bones, tore skin and muscle, and cut arteries, A -> B, each difference marked by how unlikely chance is to make it. A problem turns up in a few plays of a thousand, so a handful more or less is chance; when both sides played the same blows (the same seeds and counts on a body built alike), each is set against itself by the sign test on the blows that came out differently, which tells a real change from chance, and the blows only one side went wrong in are listed to replay; sides whose blows differ, as when the skin moved and the blows aim through other points, are compared as two samples (Fisher's exact test). `.\tools\compare.ps1 -Random 2000` plays 2000 blows from each of seeds 1, 2, and 3 on a commit and on the working tree and sets them side by side, and `.\tools\ab.ps1 -Random 2000` two ways in one build; 2000 blows take about a minute and a half.
- **The body in numbers** (`--dump bones,joints,tendons,vessels,ties` or `all`, then `:FRAME`, the start by default) lays the body out on one frame: each bone where it lies in body coordinates and how far each end is from where it was built, each joint how far it is turned, its muscles' torque, and how far its bone's end has slipped from where it was built (or that it is out of place, loose, or torn, and then which bones it joins and how far apart they are), each tendon where its ends are, how long it is against its slack and the length it tears at, and whether it pulls or has torn, each artery, and for each bone how many of its muscle ties are torn and how far its flesh has slid off it; `flesh=N` lists each point of flesh held to bone N, how far from its place, and what ties it.
- **The mesh in a picture** (`--mesh U,V[,R][:FRAME]`) draws the skin's and the muscle's triangles round a place in body coordinates (R body heights either way, 0.04 by default) on one frame into `output\mesh_NAME_FRAME.svg`, with slivers (poorly shaped triangles, which turn over easily) in blue and triangles turned over since the body was built in red, and prints how many of each. It shows the mesh's shape where the skin tears, gapes, or shows the muscle through it.
- **A recording** (`.\tools\record.ps1 -Scenario NAME`, or `-Strike`, `-Gesture`, with `-Variant`, `-Set`, `-Try`) plays one run with `strike_scenarios --record FILE.rrd` and opens it in the [Rerun](https://rerun.io) viewer (`cargo install rerun-cli --version 0.38.1 --locked`): every point of flesh, every fiber coloured by how far it is stretched, the bones, the joints, the tool, and the blood on a timeline of frames; what the injury rules decided and what the watchdogs flagged as a log on the same timeline; and plots over the run of each fist's knock, the head's turn, the fastest tissue, the blood lost, each joint's slip and turn, each bone's load, and how far each bone's flesh has slid. Recordings go to `output\record`; open two together to see what a change did. The scenario tool records only when built with the `record` feature, which the script builds in `target\record`, so no other build compiles the Rerun SDK.

Changing the engine to see what a change does needs no rebuild for its settings, and two runs are compared by how sure each difference is:

```powershell
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only shoulder_blunt --set gravity=2.867
.\tools\ab.ps1 -B "bone_joint_hertz=30" -Only "bat_hard_swing,hammer_firm_swing" -Quick
$env:RP_TRY = "new_way"; cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only bat_hard_swing
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only "fighter_punches_the_head,fighter_punches_the_guard" --grid "bone_joint_hertz=60,30,90" --quick
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --gesture "hammer:-1.3,0.95:wait={60-lead}:lean2=on:wait={lead}:punch=right:wait=60:lean2=off:wait=200:fighters" --grid "lead=0,4,8,12,16,20,30,40,60" --grid "lean_back=0.35,0.21,0.30,0.40" --grid "lean_hip_shift=0.25,0.15" --quick
```

- **Settings** (`--set NAME=VALUE[,NAME=VALUE...]`, repeatable, on the scenario tool and the viewer) change any of the engine's `Materials` from the default for every world played, such as `gravity`, `bone_joint_hertz`, or `substeps`; a misspelled name lists the names it is close to. `Materials::NAMES` lists them all, and `every_setting_has_a_name` fails if a new one is left out.
- **Experiments**: code tried out behind `trying("name")` (`realistic_physics::trying`) runs when `RP_TRY` names it, so the old way and the new can be played from one build. The test `nothing_is_left_being_tried` fails while any code still asks, so a branch reaches `main` with one way chosen.
- **A/B** (`.\tools\ab.ps1`, with `-A` and `-B` settings, `-TryA` and `-TryB` experiments, `-Only`, and `-Quick`, or `-Fights N` for random fights instead, with `-Seed` and `-Mix`) plays the sweep both ways and sets them side by side with `strike_scenarios --diff A B`: every band, real-life check, watchdog, and main number that moved, each marked by how unlikely chance is to move it as far (Fisher's exact test on counts, the sign test on numbers over the same runs). Forty-five runs are few: 20 of 45 against 24 of 45 is within chance, 5 of 1305 against 33 of 1305 is not. Every sweep writes its runs one to a line for this (`strike_sweep_runs.csv`), and `compare.ps1 -Sweep` sets a commit's sweep against the working tree's the same way. `--quick` sweeps 9 runs a scenario instead of 45, to see which way a change goes in a fifth of the time.
- **A grid** (`--grid NAME=V1,V2,...`, repeatable) answers "which values work?" in one command: it plays the scenarios `--only` names, or a custom swing or gesture, over every combination of the values given and prints one table with a row for each combination (`output\strike_grid.txt`): its runs in band, the runs that touched the body, each real-life check (`!` where it misses real life), the main numbers, and each watchdog that tripped. A column every row has alike is named once under the table instead, and with several scenarios a table over all of them comes first. NAME is a setting, as `--set` takes it, or a blank `{NAME}` in the custom play, which may hold a sum: in the last command above, step 4's lean grid, `wait={60-lead}:lean2=on:wait={lead}:punch=right` starts the right fighter's lean `lead` steps before a punch that always comes on step 60. Each row is played once (the play as tuned, or `--variant`'s run), or 9 runs with `--quick` and 45 with `--sweep`, and then every row is set against row 0 with the A/B tests (`*` under 5%, `**` under 1%), so list the value to compare against first. Each row's runs go to `output\grid\rowNN`, for `--diff` to set any two side by side in full, and the table to `output\strike_grid.csv`. That lean grid, 72 rows, takes about half a minute played once a row and four minutes with `--quick`.
- **Borderline checks**: the sweep report lists the real-life checks whose frequency could fall either side of its limit with a few runs more or fewer (the 95% Wilson interval spans the limit), passing or not, so a change that tips one over is no surprise.
- **Property tests** check the engine's geometry and solver math on many generated cases with proptest, which shrinks a failing case to the simplest one before reporting it: two segments' closest points, angles wrapping into one turn, a soft constraint never pushing harder than a rigid one, a joint's hold leading back to its anchor, and a joint's pin moving its anchor exactly as its effective mass says.

## Sound

The game's sound comes from the simulation, with no recordings or samples. `src/sound.rs` listens to the world after every step and reads what the video foley reads from `--events`: the tool's speed and whether it touches the body, and the running counts of broken bones, torn skin and muscle, and blood drops. From those it hears:

- a thud when a bat or sledgehammer lands, as loud and deep as the blow was fast (silent below 150 px/s, full strength at 2550 px/s), so the speed ladder sounds like one: a slow push is a faint tap and a full swing a heavy blow;
- a crack when a bone breaks, a wet tear when a blow tears skin or muscle, and a spatter when a cut artery's spurting blood lands (blood that wells out of a wound stays quiet);
- air rushing past a tool swung with the button down, louder and brighter as it goes faster, thinner for the knife;
- a slice while the knife cuts, as loud as the fibers it severs each step.

A sound plays no sooner than 60 to 80 ms after the last of its kind, unless it is much stronger, so a crush that breaks bone after bone does not stack into noise. `src/sound/synth.rs` synthesizes the 26 clips (three takes of each one-off sound, the thud at three strengths) in about 40 ms when the app starts, rebuilt from `video_tools\tools\foley.py`, which makes the sound of the project's videos, so the game and the videos sound alike; the air and the knife are loops whose volume follows the action. Everything plays through macroquad's audio, natively and in the browser, and a soft clip rounds off peaks when several loud sounds land at once. Sound only reads the world, so it never changes what the simulation does.

To hear a scenario without playing it, or check how loud things are:

```powershell
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --sound output\sound
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --only hammer_firm_swing --sound output\sound
cargo run --release --bin strike_scenarios -- --gesture "knife:-0.3,0.66:wait=10:down:0.1,0.66/8:wait=40:up" --sound output\sound
```

`--sound DIR` plays the scenarios (or a custom swing or gesture) and mixes what the app would play, step by step, into `DIR\NAME.wav`, writes each clip of the sound bank to `DIR\bank`, and lists every scenario's sounds, strongest strength, and levels in `DIR\sound_report.txt`. A full sledgehammer blow reaches about -16 LUFS over its loudest 400 ms, a knife cut -22, and a slow push -37.

To check that the desktop app's sound really reaches the speakers:

```powershell
.\tools\sound_check.ps1
```

It opens `realistic_physics.exe` for a few seconds, presses `H` and swings the sledgehammer by sending messages to the app's window (the real mouse and keyboard are left alone), mutes and swings again, and reads the app's own level meter in the Windows mixer the whole time. It passes when the app is silent at rest, clicks, thuds, and is silent again while muted.

macroquad's sound backend, quad-snd, is used from a patched copy in `vendor\quad-snd` (MIT; `Cargo.toml` points Cargo at it). The patches, listed in `vendor\quad-snd\PATCHES.md`, cut the Windows output latency from 83-93 ms to 13-23 ms, glide volume changes so they do not click, fix a glitch at every loop point, add the soft clip, and make browser audio start on the first tap on phones too. Its mixer tests run with `cargo test --manifest-path vendor\quad-snd\Cargo.toml --target-dir target\vendor`.

## Anatomy Diagnostics

Use this whenever changing body generation, anatomy layers, bones, constraints, or rendering assumptions:

```powershell
.\tools\verify.ps1
```

Open `output\anatomy_debug.svg` to inspect the generated body without launching the app. Skin is translucent, muscle is red, major vessels are dark red, bones are pale, ribs are slightly warmer, muscle-to-bone attachments are blue, bone joints are yellow, and bone sample markers turn red if they fall outside the skin mesh. The diagnostic exits nonzero if sampled bone centerlines are outside skin.

## Visual Damage Diagnostics

Use this whenever changing damage rendering, wound detail, fluid appearance, fracture display, or visual assumptions:

```powershell
.\tools\verify.ps1
```

Open `output\damage_visual_debug.svg` to inspect three deterministic damage captures side by side: a knife cut on the belly, a long-settle sledgehammer blow to the chest, and a knife cut down the inside of the arm that opens the artery there; neither cut may break a bone. The diagnostic also writes primitive counts to:

```text
output\damage_visual_summary.csv
```

The visual diagnostic exits nonzero if what is drawn stops matching what the simulation did: more blood shown than the wounds let out (`blood_shown_ml` against `blood_shed_ml`), more let out than the body lost, a drop's blood unaccounted for, or a piece of broken bone outside the body. It also exits nonzero if the injuries a play really causes stop showing: the cuts' wound edges, incision lines, and opened insides, the bruised and crushed flesh, the cut artery, and the sledgehammer blow's fractures, fracture caps, cavity pressure, and organ injury where each play causes them. The gates follow what each play does in real life, so the belly cut need not reach a vessel and the sledgehammer blow need not break a rib, and none asks for a set amount of blood: a wound shows the blood it lost, and blood a closed injury keeps in the flesh shows as a bruise. Where each wound bleeds from is drawn too, as a debugging aid, filled where its blood can get out and as a ring under unbroken skin; the app shows these only in its debug view.

## Development Notes

- `Cargo.toml` defines the Rust library, app, diagnostics, strike scenario, contact sheet, and icon binaries.
- `src/simulation.rs` contains the physics data model, integration, constraints, tearing, bone fracture, major vessels, wounds, and fluid particles; `src/simulation/body.rs` generates the layered body; `src/simulation/tools.rs` holds the tools: their shapes, the hand that drives them, and their contact with tissue and bone; `src/simulation/cuts.rs` opens the cuts a blade makes by splitting the tissue's points along them; `src/simulation/soft.rs` is the solver that moves the tissue and bones, rebuilt from Box2D v3's soft step; `src/simulation/tissue.rs` weighs the tissue and sets its stiffness from what skin, fat, and muscle measure; `src/simulation/blood.rs` holds blood out of the body, sized by its volume: drops, the beads and trails on the skin, and pools on the floor; `src/simulation/bruises.rs` colours the skin as a blow's redness fades and its bruise darkens over hours; `src/simulation/outline.rs` keeps body parts from passing through each other; `src/simulation/grid.rs` is the spatial grid the bone fragment contacts and the outline contact search use (rebuilt with a counting sort into reused arrays, and searched in a fixed order, since the contact passes resolve contacts one after another); `src/simulation/phases.rs` times each phase of a step for `strike_scenarios --phases` and follows one point or bone through them for `--trace`; `src/simulation/punch.rs` throws a fighter's punches and `src/simulation/lean.rs` leans it back while it holds a key, each moving the trunk and limbs as pieces of bone and flesh.
- `src/view.rs` says where every body is simulated (`world_frame`, the scenarios' placement in a 1280x720 world) and holds `View`, the scale and offset that draw the world on a screen and turn the pointer back into world positions; the app, capture mode, and the viewer draw through it, so no window changes what happens to the body. `fighters_across` fits two fighters across a window.
- `src/draw.rs` and `src/draw/` draw the world with `macroquad`: the bodies' flesh, bones, and wounds, blood, and the tool in hand, from what the simulation did. The app, capture mode, the viewer, and the fighting game built on the engine all draw through it, so they look alike; each draws its own buttons around it.
- `src/bin/realistic_physics/main.rs` owns the `macroquad` app shell: input, timing, and the HUD and buttons. It also runs in the browser, so the app and simulation must avoid file I/O, threads, and `std::time`, none of which work on `wasm32-unknown-unknown`. `src/bin/realistic_physics/capture.rs` is the native-only screenshot mode, and `src/bin/realistic_physics/selftest.rs` the self-test mode, which runs in both builds (the page hands it a clock and takes its report through a small gl.js plugin). `src/bin/realistic_physics/about.rs` shows the about card through the same plugin, or natively opens the write-up in the default browser.
- `src/scenarios.rs` holds the scripted strikes and gestures and the tuned scenarios with their injury and steadiness bands, shared by the strike runner, the visual damage diagnostic, the capture mode, and the self-test, which also ships in the browser build.
- `src/sound.rs` hears what each simulation step sounded like and picks the clip and volume for each sound; `src/sound/synth.rs` synthesizes the clips and `src/sound/mixdown.rs` mixes a run offline the way the app plays it. `src/sound/player.rs` plays it through `macroquad` in the app and the game, and `tests/sound_tests.rs` checks it. `vendor/quad-snd` is the patched sound backend.
- `src/version.rs` writes the version as the game shows it (v0.7 for 0.7.0), and its test checks that `CHANGELOG.md` starts with that version's entry (see [Versions](#versions)).
- `web/index.html` is the browser page: start screen with a content warning, the version and what is new in it (which `tools/build_web.ps1` fills in from `Cargo.toml` and `CHANGELOG.md`), and an About section with links (the same card opens again as the about card), loader, error messages, and the `?stats` frame-time overlay (also exposed as `window.__perf` for automated checks). `web/analytics.js` counts visits on the live site (see [Visit Counts](#visit-counts)); `src/bin/realistic_physics/analytics.rs` tells it the first blow with each tool, through an import the page's gl.js plugin defines. `web/manifest.webmanifest` and `web/icons` let visitors install the page as an app, and the start screen's install section tells them how (see [Install It As An App](#install-it-as-an-app)).
- `tools/build_web.ps1` builds the browser version into `target\web`, with `-Fight PATH` the Fight page from a checkout of the game's repository too (see [Fight](#fight)); `tools/serve_web.ps1` serves it locally with the content types browsers require (`application/wasm`, and JSON for the manifest), which `Get-WebContentTypes` in `tools/common.ps1` lists for every local server.
- `tools/bench.ps1` times the simulation against a commit (see [Measure Speed](#measure-speed)).
- `.cargo/config.toml` lets the wasm linker leave miniquad's WebGL functions as imports for `gl.js` to provide; recent Rust versions no longer do that by default.
- Every text file has Unix line endings (LF), in the repository and in every checkout: `.gitattributes` says so whatever Git for Windows' `core.autocrlf` asks for, and `rustfmt.toml` has `cargo fmt` write them. With Windows line endings (CRLF) on disk, the tools that write files (`cargo fmt`, `sed -i`, scripts) put LF into them and mixed the two. A checkout made before `.gitattributes` still holds CRLF files: `.\tools\line_endings.ps1` switches them without changing what git sees in them, uncommitted changes included (`-Check` only lists them, `-Path` switches another checkout), and `verify.ps1` runs it first.
- `src/bin/anatomy_diagnostics.rs` writes a deterministic SVG anatomy snapshot and reports geometry validation metrics.
- `src/bin/strike_scenarios.rs` writes deterministic strike telemetry and tuning summaries, sweeps scenarios over small swing offsets, plays custom swings, and times the phases of a step.
- `src/bin/visual_damage_diagnostics.rs` writes deterministic SVG damage captures and visual primitive metrics.
- `src/bin/contact_sheet.rs` lays PNG screenshots out in one image.
- `src/bin/make_icons.rs` draws the browser version's app icons from the mannequin's signed distance field (`silhouette_distance`) and the sledgehammer's shape.
- `tools/merge_check.ps1`, `tools/verify.ps1`, `tools/compare.ps1`, `tools/parity.ps1`, `tools/touch_check.ps1`, `tools/analytics_check.ps1`, `tools/install_check.ps1`, `tools/capture.ps1`, and `tools/sound_check.ps1` are the checking workflows described above; `tools/headless.ps1` drives the headless browser for the parity, touch, analytics, and install checks.
- `tests/simulation_tests.rs` contains focused Rust simulation checks, including mirror symmetry of the body and skin covering the muscle at rest.
- `src/silhouette.rs` defines the mannequin figure (torso outline, limb joints and radii, head, hands, feet) for one side and mirrors it into a signed distance field; `src/simulation/body.rs` meshes the body from it, builds the limb bones on the same joints, and places the rest of the anatomy. `body_frame` maps body coordinates (fractions of body height) into a window, which the strike scenarios use to aim at anatomy. Every body is simulated at the scenarios' `body_frame` (`world_frame`); the app draws it at `body_frame` for its own window, or at `body_frame_between` when that placement would run under its status chips or control buttons.

The next Rust simulation milestones are:

1. Expand the Rust test matrix to cover more strike and fracture edge cases.
2. Give muscle strain-based triangles in place of its fiber, cross, and shear springs, and let tissue tear by the stress pulling it in each direction, on the physical tissue.
3. Tighten strike tuning bands once material behavior has settled enough for intentional regression gates.
4. Keep increasing fracture density in small measured steps, using the long-settle telemetry to catch fragment sleep, budget, and stability regressions.
5. Verify the `macroquad` app on macOS and document any platform-specific packaging steps.
6. Add a pixel-level comparison on top of the app captures, so rendering regressions against a baseline commit show up without looking at the images.

## Toolchain

The primary build now uses Rust and Cargo. The app frontend uses `macroquad` for a cross-platform window, input, sound, and 2D drawing path while the simulation stays in a reusable Rust library. The browser version is the same app compiled for `wasm32-unknown-unknown`, drawn with WebGL and heard through Web Audio. The library also depends on `libm`, for trigonometry that gives the same bits in both builds (see [Check Browser Parity](#check-browser-parity)).
