# Injury reference

The strike scenarios (`src/scenarios.rs`) check what the simulation does against what blows, cuts, and bleeding do to real people, from published biomechanics, forensic, and medical research. This file holds that research. Each scenario's `real` list names the outcome, how often it happens in real life, and the source; the rules every scenario follows are under [Rules for every play](#rules-for-every-play).

Figures marked *checked* were read in the source itself while writing these tests. The others come from the research summaries behind them and were not re-read.

## Scale

- The figure stands for an adult 1.75 m tall, weighing about 70 kg. In the scenarios' 1280x720 window it is 561.6 px tall, so one pixel is about 3.1 mm and 1,000 px/s is about 3.1 m/s (`scenario_metres_per_px`).
- Blood: about 70 ml per kg of body weight, so about 4.9 L (the trauma-care figure, as cited in Gutierrez et al. 2004). The engine's blood loss is a share of that volume (`BLOOD_VOLUME_ML`).
- Tools: a 4.5 kg (10 lb) sledgehammer head and a 0.8 kg bat (the 0.79 kg, 0.71 m bat in Guo et al. 2026). The speed of a tool is the speed of its striking part: the hammer's head, the bat's barrel, the knife's blade. A bat turns about the hands on its handle, so its barrel meets what it strikes with less than the whole bat's mass, 1 / (1 / M + b² / I): about 0.6 kg for a 0.8 kg bat struck 15 cm past its centre of mass, where I is about 0.045 kg m². A sledgehammer's head strikes with all of its mass. A bare fist strikes with the 2.9 kg a punch carries ([Punches](#punches)).
- Top speeds in the engine (`max_speed` in `src/simulation/tools.rs`): a knife 4,800 px/s (about 15 m/s, the fastest slash measured), a sledgehammer 3,400 px/s (about 10.6 m/s), a bat's barrel 8,000 px/s (about 25 m/s, between an ordinary man's swing and a trained hitter's), and a fist 3,200 px/s (about 10 m/s, toward the top of untrained men's hardest punches).

## Bones

Bats break the forearm most: of 146 fractures in 116 people struck with bats, 61 were of the ulna, 27 of the hand, and 14 of the radius, and 20% were open (Bryant et al. 1992, ["Musculoskeletal trauma: the baseball bat"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2571736/), *checked*). Bat blows also break the shin (Levy et al. 1994, 11 cases, *checked*).

**Energy estimates.** The scenarios compare a blow's energy with each bone's limit. For a given contact, the peak force of a blow grows with the square root of its energy, so a bone that takes k times a forearm's breaking force takes about k² times its energy. With the forearm at about 20 J and each bone's force from its bending limit over its length, that gives roughly: collarbone 5 J, forearm 20 J, upper arm 90 J, shin 100 J, thigh 120 J. These are estimates for comparing blows, not measurements.

**How often a blow breaks a bone.** A scenario's check follows its blow's energy against the bone's estimate: 4 times or more, always; 2-4 times, usually; 0.5-2 times, sometimes; 0.25-0.5 times, rarely; below that, never. The bands are wide because the estimates are rough and bones differ by 15-28% from person to person.

**In the engine.** Each bone is as strong as the bending tests make it against the forearm (`bone_strength` in `src/simulation/body.rs`): the collarbone about half, the upper arm 2.1 times, the shin 2.25, the thigh 2.5. The forearm is set 30% above its share, inside the spread of the forearm studies, so a light bat swing rarely breaks it. How hard a blow presses on a bone follows from its speed, the masses that meet, and the flesh over the bone ([How blows reach bone](#how-blows-reach-bone)). Only blows, pieces of broken bone, and flesh pressed onto a bone break it; what a joint passes on strains the joint but breaks no bone.

**Where a bone breaks.** A bone breaks where a load bends it most, and a load bends a bone by its lever on what holds the bone, not by its force alone. In the tests above the bone rests on two supports and is struck between them, which bends it by F a b / L under the blow, with a and b the blow's distances from the supports and L the span: most in the middle, so the thigh bones of Kennedy et al., struck in the middle, all broke there, under the impactor. In the body a long bone rests on its joints the same way, so a blow a quarter of the way along a bone bends it 0.75 times as hard as the same blow in the middle, and one a tenth of the way 0.36 times; a blow pressed along a stretch of the bone, as a bat lying along a forearm, bends it less than one at a point, half as hard when spread over the whole span. Past a bone's last joint, as a hand's fingers, only the bone's own inertia holds it, so a blow there turns it more than it bends it. A bending break can also break a wedge out of the bone, a butterfly fragment: classically its broad side lies where the blow landed (Messerer's wedge), though bending tests find wedges in only some breaks, on either side (Isa et al. 2021).

**In the engine.** Every load that can break a bone counts by how hard it bends the bone where it lands, against the same load at one point in the middle of a span between two joints, and the bone breaks where it was bent hardest (`src/simulation/bending.rs`). A break twice the bone's strength or more also breaks a wedge out of the struck side. A load along a bone and a blow on a bone's end count as [How blows reach bone](#how-blows-reach-bone) describes. Each collarbone is its own bone, from the top of the breastbone out to its shoulder, so one breaks without moving the other shoulder; it lifts at the breastbone by up to about 31 degrees and drops by up to about 11 (in life it lifts about 45 and drops about 10).

## How blows reach bone

A blunt blow presses on a bone with the peak force of a mass meeting a spring: F = v √(k m), where v is the speed at which the tool meets the bone, k the stiffness of the contact, and m the tool's and the struck part's masses in series, m_tool m_part / (m_tool + m_part). Robinovitch, Hayes, and McMahon built this model for falls on the hip ([J Biomech Eng 113:366, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.2895414)). The force grows with the speed of a blow rather than its energy, so a bone k times as strong takes a blow k² times as energetic to break, as the energy estimates above assume.

The flesh over a bone is a spring in series with it, so a thickness h of it softens the contact to k₀ / (1 + h / h₀) and the force to F₀ / √(1 + h / h₀). Robinovitch, McMahon, and Hayes found thicker hip tissue gave a lower peak force under a 140 J impact ([J Orthop Res 13:956, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1002/jor.1100130621), *checked (abstract)*); applied as a clinical rule, that is about 71 N less a millimetre, 27% less at 30 mm ([Nielson et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2008-1640), *checked*). Fitting the spring to it puts h₀ at 7-34 mm; the engine takes 15 mm, and at least 40% of a blow reaches a bone however thick its cover, since a hard blow presses the flesh flat (the thickest tissue still passed 63% of the thinnest's force).

The figure is a flat front view, so the flesh drawn between a tool and a bone is not always what covers it: above the collarbone it draws the trapezius, which in a real shoulder lies behind the collarbone, so the figure has 26 mm over the outer collarbone where a person has about 8. A bone therefore takes a blow from the moment the tool presses the skin over it in the figure, measured from the figure's outline when the body is built, and the real thickness above sets how much that flesh softens it. `anatomy_diagnostics` prints the two side by side for each bone.

The struck part's mass is what a blow across its middle meets: a limb segment turns about the joint above it with the limb beyond it held straight, so from Winter's segment masses, lengths, and radii of gyration (*Biomechanics and Motor Control of Human Movement*, 2009) a blow meets 4.5 kg at the forearm, 18 at the upper arm, 8.7 at the shin, and 39 at the thigh; the legforms that stand in for a pedestrian's leg in crash tests weigh 8.6 kg above the knee and 4.8 below (EU Directive 2004/90). The collarbone and ribs are taken as braced on the trunk, 10 kg. A light bat meets a limb with nearly all its mass, while much of a heavy hammer's goes into shoving the limb aside.

The stiffness of a blow on bare bone, k₀, is set so blows break bones as often as the research says, as each tool's break scale was before: 600 kN/m. The 4.5 kg weight that broke forearms held at both ends at 3 m/s and 1.37 kN met them through about 46 kN/m; a blow in the figure lands on a free limb, often off the middle and spread along it, so it takes a stiffer contact to break the same bones.

**Along the bone and at its ends.** A long bone takes several times the force along its length that breaks it across: the lower leg 4.3-11.4 kN under fast loading (Yoganandan et al. 1996, SAE 962426, *from a summary*) against 1-4 kN across, and the curved collarbone about twice, 1.48 ± 0.46 kN (Duprey et al. 2008, [J Biomech 41:200](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2007.06.020), *checked (abstract)*) and 2.41 ± 0.72 kN (Arregui-Dalmases et al. 2010, [Traffic Inj Prev 11:286](https://doi.org/10.1080/15389581003709902), *checked (abstract)*) against 0.73 kN across (Kemper et al. 2006) and 1.16 ± 0.13 kN front to back (Untaroiu et al. 2009, *checked (abstract)*). The two add, as in the Tibia Index (Mertz 1993). The end of a bone beside a joint can break under a blow there: side impacts at the knee broke the joints' surfaces at 2.4 ± 0.7 kN where thigh bones broke at 1.4 kN (Kajzer et al. 1997 and 1999, SAE 973326 and 1999-01-0712, *from a summary*), and 28% of broken collarbones break in their outer fifth, against 69% in the middle and 3% at the inner end (Robinson 1998, [J Bone Joint Surg Br 80:476](https://doi.org/10.1302/0301-620X.80B3.0800476), *from a summary*).

**In the engine.** A load along a long bone counts a quarter as much as one across it, along a collarbone half, and pressed along its length a bone gives way in the middle of its span; the figure's rib is the half of a real one from the spine round to the side, so a blow along it strikes the side of the chest across the real rib and counts whole. A blow on the end of a long bone, its last 15% (about as long as the end is wide, the AO/OTA square rule, Meinberg et al. 2018), or on the outer fifth of a collarbone crushes the end as 0.6 of the blow, so it takes about 1.7 times the force that breaks the shaft across its middle. A bone takes the blow over the stretch the tool presses at the blow's peak, pressed in as far as the flesh and bone give, v √(m / k).

**Open fractures.** How often a break comes out through the skin depends on the weapon:

| Weapon | Open | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Bat | A fifth of 146 fractures | Bryant et al. 1992 (above). *Checked.* |
| Sticks and clubs | 44% of 64 fractures in 28 people | Nolan et al. 2000, ["The price of peace"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10716049/). *Checked (abstract).* |
| Iron bars or similar | 70% of 18 people had an open fracture (a share of people, not of fractures) | Eames et al. 1997, ["A fractured peace"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9326144/). *Checked (abstract).* |

In the engine a break comes out open only when the blow goes far enough past the bone's strength, by tool (`open_fracture_scale`): furthest for a bat's smooth, rounded barrel, less for a sledgehammer. The pieces of a closed break do not cut the skin from inside; those of an open break, and splinters, do. Whole skin holds a closed break's pieces in, as skin tents over a broken bone it is not pierced by: a piece's end or middle found just outside the skin round its own limb is pushed back in and the skin out over it (`hold_closed_pieces_in_the_skin`); where that skin is torn or cut, nothing holds it. Held by the nearest skin of any part, a smashed arm's pieces were held against the head, and the arm with them.

**Knives.** Pocket knives pierce ribs at 11-16 J (above 0.9-1.2 kN), and a hard stab carries 64-115 J (Bolliger et al. 2016, ["Stabbing energy and force required for pocket-knives to pierce ribs"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12024-016-9803-z), *checked*; Horsfall et al. 1999). No study found an ordinary knife breaking a long bone; heavy chopping blades do (Lynn and Fairgrieve 2009, axes and hatchets; Gentile et al. 2019, about 15 J for an axe on a forearm-sized bone).

**In the engine.** The figure is a flat front view, so each sheet stands for a slab of the body seen from the front. The skin sheet is the skin and the fat under it, which slide over the muscle together and run round a limb about three times as far as the front view shows; the muscle sheet is everything under them, as deep as the body is there (a limb about round, the trunk about three quarters as deep as wide). Each point weighs its share of its sheet's triangles at those densities, and each bone its part's share of the body times its share of bone. A sheet of shear modulus μ and thickness t resists in its plane with μt newtons per metre; a triangle mesh carries that on springs of 8/3 μt times the area of each side's triangles over its length squared (Van Gelder 1998), and the rest of its resistance to growing or shrinking, as tissue that keeps its volume does, on each triangle's area. Skin shears at 0.2 MPa, a third of living skin's Young's modulus, is 2 mm thick, and is 70 times stiffer past a quarter's stretch; muscle shears at 4 kPa along its fibers and 6 across, and is 12 and 4 times stiffer past 15% and 10%; the fat, 10 mm thick, shears at 7.5 kPa, up to 6 times stiffer once it has slid half its thickness, and tears once it has slid six thicknesses, or one and a half under a crushing load. Skin rests stretched 8% every way, the smaller of the two measured stretches: with no back to wrap round, the flat sheet did not balance when pulled harder round the limbs than along them. The body's outline holds the stretch, as the skin running on round the back of a real body does, so a cut gapes and torn skin pulls back on its own. Tissue rings with a damping ratio of 0.3 (the fat 0.5), and muscle springs back onto its bone at 30 Hz, within the 10-55 Hz soft tissue rings at after a jolt and toward its top, as braced muscle is (at 20 Hz a blow that turned the guard's forearm about its pinned elbow left the bone outside its flesh), and the foot's flesh, bound to its bones by fascia over little muscle, at 55 Hz, the top of the measured range. Tears are judged by how far tissue is stretched past where the living body holds it. Set by judgement, not measured: how firmly the body holds its pose as it hangs (1-1.7 Hz), how stiff its joints are (60 Hz, as Box2D holds its joints), and how far flesh slips off its bone before the tie tears (40 mm, or 15 under load).

A joint's damping is its stiffness times 0.05 s, which settles a struck limb in well under a second. An arm held against a push springs back toward where it was held (Bennett et al. 1992), which `bat_tap_on_the_guard` checks. With a broken collarbone, upper arm, or forearm a person cannot hold the arm up and holds it against the body (Pecci and Kreher 2008, *Am Fam Physician*; AAOS OrthoInfo), and a broken thigh or shin bears no weight, so the muscles of a limb with one of those broken let go: its joints hold no pose and keep a quarter of their damping, since a relaxed joint is damped a few times less than a tensed one (0.76 N·m·s/rad at the relaxed elbow, Gomi and Osu 1998). `bat_hard_forearm` checks that the arm drops out of the guard.

**In the engine.** The muscles across a joint are rebuilt from Box2D's motor joint (Erin Catto, MIT, `motor_joint.c`): a soft spring toward the guard's angle whose torque is capped at the joint's strength, warm started from what it gave a substep before. Its stiffness and damping are given in newton metres per radian and newton metre seconds per radian, as Jolt's spring parts take them (Jorrit Rouwe, MIT, `SpringPart.h`), so a joint is as stiff as the measured one however light its bones are beside the flesh they carry, capped as Box2D caps a joint at a quarter of the substep rate on its bones' own mass. The body does not keep its own balance yet, so each end of the pelvis is held where it stood, as firmly as legs hold up a body (the whole body on a 6 Hz spring, critically damped), and the floor lies at the soles. Every point of skin and muscle keeps its place on the bones under it, the two nearest counting by how near they lie (skin 1.05 Hz, muscle 1.65 Hz, set by judgement), and the bones take the pull back, so the flesh's weight hangs on them. Gravity is the standard 9.81 m/s² at the body's own scale, however large it is drawn (`STANDARD_GRAVITY`, turned into pixels by `World::gravity_px`), so a limb that lets go falls as fast as a real one. Each joint holds its second bone's end where it was built on the first, as Box2D's revolute joint holds its two anchors together, so a bone turns about its joint but cannot swing round it; a joint that held only the gap between its bones let a collarbone slide down beside the spine and the head tip off its top. The body is built in the guard without its weight and settles under it for a second and a half before anything happens to it, taking the weight over the first half second: given it all at once, the trunk dropped onto the pelvis before the joints had learned to carry it, and on a body drawn larger tore the lower back. Meanwhile its muscles learn the guard's weight, as a person sets the muscles holding a limb for the load it carries so that it rests where they mean it to (Feldman 1986, the equilibrium-point model); each joint learns how far it gives under what it carries, a few pixels for a 60 Hz joint where a real one gives under a millimetre; and the hips' hold learns how far the body sinks as a whole, never how the pelvis tilts, since the trunk stands on the pelvis like an upturned pendulum, which a hold that learned the tilt rocked over within six seconds. So the body rests in the guard as drawn, each bone within about 2 px of its place. A joint torn apart, or loosened by a break at its end, holds its bones as a slack rope that lets their ends part about 3 cm past their rest gap (10 px, at least 2.6 times the gap), set by judgement: a dislocated joint's ends lie about a joint's width apart, held there by its capsule and the muscles across it; with 11 cm of slack a smashed arm hung its bones below its flesh.

## The muscles across the elbow

The muscles that cross the elbow keep a forearm hanging from the upper arm when the elbow is torn or the forearm smashed: a mangled arm stays one arm, its bones inside it. They are slack through the elbow's whole range, so they leave a limp arm free to hang and an arm free to straighten, and pull only once the forearm is pulled off the upper arm further than the range lets it go.

**In the engine.** Each muscle is a straight cord between its places on the two bones (`src/simulation/tendons.rs`), rebuilt from MuJoCo's spatial tendon with a length limit (Google DeepMind, Apache-2.0) and solved as Box2D v3's distance joint solves its upper limit (Erin Catto, MIT): while slack it only keeps the bones from passing its length within a substep, and once taut it pulls as stiffly as a joint, but no harder than its muscle's strength, as Box2D's motor joint holds no harder than its greatest force. Its slack is the longest it gets as the forearm turns through the elbow's whole range, plus 1 cm for the relaxed muscle's give, and it tears once stretched past that by 60% of its fibres' length, where Thelen's relaxed fibres would bear its full strength: for the brachialis 37% past the length of its fibres and tendon together, about where the rabbit's tore. A cord that held whatever it took, stretched across a smashed upper arm and forearm, hauled the arm's top piece down out of its torn shoulder and tipped the trunk over. Pulling as softly as a relaxed muscle does, the cords let a smashed arm's pieces drift out of the body (11 sweep runs, against 6 without the cords), the flesh alone not holding them; the firm cord stands in for the fascia and the rest of what holds a mangled limb together (3 runs). When a bone breaks, each cord's end stays with the piece that carries its place. The forearm's flexors and extensors, which run on from the epicondyles to the hand, are left out: pulled by them, the hand's bone came out of the fist's flesh when a smashed forearm hung on them. The inside of each elbow is a crease, as in a real arm: where the guard folds the forearm against the upper arm their flesh meets without being one piece, so the elbow opens freely; filled with one block of flesh, the fold held a broken arm up in the guard like a wedge of rubber and tore once the elbow straightened. The flesh over the point of the elbow, past the ends of its bones, slides over the joint rather than being tied to either bone: tied to the end of the upper arm, it was torn off as the elbow straightened. The flesh round each elbow rests as a relaxed elbow's does, 61 degrees more open than the guard (`elbow_rest_opening`, `rest_flesh_round_joints`): while the body is built, a weightless copy of it opens each elbow to the relaxed angle and on to straight, and the flesh within four point spacings of the elbow takes the lengths it had relaxed and tears only past the longest the range stretched it. Rested in the guard, the fold held a limp forearm up. Turned by stiff muscles with no weight to carry, the elbow's flesh now resists opening with about 1.2 N·m per radian, within the measured range, and holds no torque about 40 degrees past the guard, where the flesh further from the elbow, which rests as built, pulls back as hard as the relaxed flesh pushes on.

## Rules for every play

- **Bleeding follows what was cut.** Wounds that miss arteries bleed at most 10 ml a minute each (grades 1-2), an open fracture's marrow at most 50, and a cut artery at least 10. The rate is the average since the body first lost blood (`ScenarioResult::bleeding_ml_per_min`), shared among the wounds open at once unless an artery is cut, since the scale grades each bleeding site.
- **A knife never breaks a long bone** (collarbone, upper arm, forearm, thigh, shin).
- **Broken bone stays in the body.** No piece of broken bone may end up more than three point spacings (about 11 cm) from all flesh (`bone_pieces_out`); at worst a broken end pokes out through the skin. A segment thrown clear of a limb comes only from high-energy injuries (Hu et al. 2024). A closed break's pieces belong inside the skin, as does every whole bone; the sweep reports how often one ends up outside it (`closed_pieces_out`, `whole_bones_out`), since a hard blow can still squeeze the flat body's flesh off a bone, but does not yet fail a run for it.
- **Struck flesh moves at most twice as fast as the tool that hit it,** the limit of a perfectly elastic collision; faster flesh means the simulation flung it. When the figure punches, its fist counts as the tool, since its own flesh moves as fast as its fist.

## How the scenarios use this

- Each scenario whose play can injure lists what it does in real life, and every scenario follows the rules above. Each check says how often an outcome happens: never, rarely (in at most a third of runs), sometimes (in some runs but not all), usually (in at least half), or always.
- One run can only judge never and always. The sweep (`strike_scenarios --sweep`) judges the rest over 45 replays of each scenario: the swing moved slightly along and across its path, on bones 20% weaker, as tuned, and 25% stronger, since real bones differ that much between people. A fight's sweep moves the fighters up to 0.01 body heights nearer or further apart and the punch's aim up to 0.015 rad lower or higher instead, as people stand and punch a little differently.
- Counts with no real-world meaning, like torn mesh links or skin flaps, are not checked. `compare.ps1` still reports every change in them, so a change can be looked at in captures.
- A check the engine cannot meet yet is marked as a known gap, with why. The sweep reports known gaps on their own and does not count them as mismatches. One is open: a punch on the guard drops the arm ([Punches](#punches)). The two before it closed when blows began to reach bone through the flesh over it ([How blows reach bone](#how-blows-reach-bone)): a bat brought down on the shoulder never broke the collarbone, because the figure draws more flesh over it than a real shoulder has, and a hard bat swing into the shin broke it every time, often in three places, because the force a blow put on bone grew with its energy rather than its speed, so the hardest swings overloaded the strongest bones.
